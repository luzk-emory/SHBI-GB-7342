\begin{frame}{The Coffee Chain, Before the Experiment}

Lena runs customer analytics at Houtan Coffee, a coffee chain with 10{,}000 loyalty members.

\bigskip

Marketing wants to send every member a coupon: \textyen 10 off the next purchase.

\bigskip

\textbf{Their evidence:} a Q1 pilot in one region of 1{,}000 members. The coupon went to 500 of them, the ones marketing judged most loyal.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Got coupon} & \textbf{No coupon} \\
\midrule
Purchase rate & 0.68 & 0.24 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\begin{center}
Marketing's reading: \emph{``The coupon lifts purchases by 44 points. Send it to everyone.''}
\end{center}

\medskip

\small Before judging the 44 points we need three things: what the number is supposed to measure, what a coupon is worth, and how the 500 were chosen.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two Worlds for Every Customer}

For each customer $i = 1, \dots, n$:

\medskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
$D_i \in \{0, 1\}$ & treatment: 1 = coupon, 0 = no coupon \\[0.3em]
$Y_i(1)$ & \textbf{potential outcome} with the coupon (buys: 1, does not: 0) \\[0.3em]
$Y_i(0)$ & \textbf{potential outcome} without it \\[0.3em]
$\tau_i = Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)$ & \textbf{individual effect}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Both potential outcomes exist before the coupon is sent. Sending it only decides which one we see.

\bigskip

A causal effect compares the same customer in two worlds, not two different customers.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Estimand: Say It in a Sentence First}

\begin{definition*}{1.1}{Estimand}
An estimand is a summary of potential outcomes for a named population. It specifies the units, the intervention and its comparator, the outcome and its horizon. It is fixed before, and independently of, any data or method.
\end{definition*}

\medskip

\emph{``The average effect of sending this \textyen 10 app coupon, against sending nothing, on a purchase within seven days, among \underline{these} customers.''}

\bigskip

\textbf{Which population?} The 1{,}000 members of the test region are where an effect can be \emph{measured}: their average effect $\tau_S = \frac{1}{n}\sum_i \tau_i$ is a fixed number, and needs no assumption about how they were sampled. The 10{,}000-member base is where the decision \emph{lands}. Moving an effect from one to the other is a separate, assumed step called transport.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Three Averages, Three Decisions}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimand} & \textbf{Definition} & \textbf{Lena's question} \\
\midrule
$\ATE$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0)]$ & coupon to everyone? \\
$\ATT$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 1]$ & keep sending it to those who get it now? \\
$\ATU$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 0]$ & extend it to those who do not? \\
$\tau(x)$ & $\E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x]$ & which customers? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The ATT and ATU depend on who was chosen; the ATE does not.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.2}{The ATE averages the ATT and ATU}
With $\pi = \Pr(D=1)$, \quad $\ATE = \pi\,\ATT + (1-\pi)\,\ATU$.
\end{result}

\small The customers are the coupon holders plus everyone else, so the overall effect is the weighted average of the two groups' effects.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R2: the warm-up records, then the pilot region's type counts (a thought experiment: nobody observes types)]
\begin{frame}{The Four Types of Customers}

With a buy-or-not outcome, each customer has one of four pairs $(Y(0), Y(1))$.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{l|c|c|}
\multicolumn{1}{c}{} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{\textbf{Doesn't buy with coupon}} & \multicolumn{1}{c}{\textbf{Buys with coupon}} \\
\cline{2-3}
\textbf{Doesn't buy without} & \cellcolor{gray!15} Lost cause & \cellcolor{green!15} Persuadable \\
& $\tau_i = 0$ & $\tau_i = +1$ \\
\cline{2-3}
\textbf{Buys without} & \cellcolor{red!15} Do-not-disturb & \cellcolor{blue!10} Sure thing \\
& $\tau_i = -1$ & $\tau_i = 0$ \\
\cline{2-3}
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Persuadable:} the coupon \emph{causes} a purchase.
    \item \textbf{Sure thing:} buys anyway; the coupon discounts a purchase that was coming.
    \item \textbf{Lost cause:} never buys; the coupon goes unused.
    \item \textbf{Do-not-disturb:} the coupon \emph{prevents} a purchase.
\end{itemize}

\small Types are a thought device: a type needs both potential outcomes, so nobody's type is ever observed.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The ATE in Response Types}

\begin{result}{1.3}{The ATE in response types}
$\ATE = \Pr(\text{persuadable}) - \Pr(\text{do-not-disturb})$.
\end{result}

\medskip

Suppose we could see the types of the pilot region's 1{,}000 members:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrc}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Count} & \textbf{Share} \\
\midrule
Lost cause (0,0) & 400 & 40\% \\
Sure thing (1,1) & 300 & 30\% \\
Persuadable (0,1) & 200 & 20\% \\
Do-not-disturb (1,0) & 100 & 10\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\ATE = 0.20 - 0.10 = \mathbf{0.10}$. Data can identify this difference, but not the two shares: 30\% and 20\% would give the same $0.10$.

\medskip

\small A model that predicts who buys ranks sure things first. They have the highest purchase rate and zero effect.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Number That Decides It}

A purchase earns $m = $ \textyen 30 of margin. The coupon costs $c = $ \textyen 10, paid on \emph{every} purchase a holder makes, including the ones they would have made anyway.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.4}{Net value and break-even}
The expected net value per unit treated, relative to not treating, is
\[
  V = m\tau - c\,p_1 = (m-c)\,\tau - c\,p_0,
\]
and treating pays if and only if $\tau > \tau^\ast = c\,p_1/m$.
\end{result}

Here $p_1 = \E[Y(1)]$, $p_0 = \E[Y(0)]$. For Lena's coupon, $\tau^\ast = 10\,p_1/30 = p_1/3$.

\medskip

Marketing's numbers: $p_1 = 0.68$, so $\tau^\ast = 0.227$. Their lift of $0.44$ gives $0.44 \times 30 - 6.80 = +$\textyen 6.40 per coupon, \textbf{+\textyen 64{,}000} across the base.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Each Type Costs}

Change in margin from sending one coupon. \textyen 30 per purchase; the \textyen 10 is paid only when the coupon is used.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Without coupon} & \textbf{With coupon} & \textbf{Change} \\
\midrule
Persuadable & 0 & 20 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{$+20$} \\
Sure thing & 30 & 20 & \textcolor{warnred}{$-10$} \\
Lost cause & 0 & 0 & $0$ \\
Do-not-disturb & 30 & 0 & \textcolor{warnred}{$-30$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The second form of Result 1.4 says the coupon earns $m - c = 20$ on each purchase it causes and loses $c = 10$ on each purchase that would have happened anyway, the \textbf{sure-thing cost} $c\,p_0$.

\medskip

\textbf{The expensive customer is the sure thing, not the lost cause.} If the coupon instead cost $c$ per customer \emph{sent} (a contact fee), then $V = m\tau - c$ and $\tau^\ast = c/m$, and lost causes would cost money too. Always say which cost model applies.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{One World Is Always Missing}

We observe
\[
Y_i = D_i\, Y_i(1) + (1 - D_i)\, Y_i(0) .
\]
Causal inference is a missing-data problem in which the missing values are missing \emph{by design}.

\medskip

\textbf{Board task.} Four customers from the pilot. Fill in only what was observed.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{crrccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Customer} & $D$ & $Y$ & $Y(1)$ & $Y(0)$ & \textbf{Compatible types} \\
\midrule
A & 1 & 1 & 1 & ? & sure thing or persuadable \\
B & 0 & 1 & ? & 1 & sure thing or do-not-disturb \\
C & 1 & 0 & 0 & ? & lost cause or do-not-disturb \\
D & 0 & 0 & ? & 0 & lost cause or persuadable \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

More customers help us estimate averages. They never reveal A's missing outcome.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Writing $Y_i(d)$ Assumes}

Writing $Y_i(d)$ relies on the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA).

\smallskip

\begin{assumption}{1.5}{SUTVA}
(i) \emph{No interference}: unit $i$'s outcome depends only on its own treatment. (ii) \emph{Consistency}: ``treated'' names one well-defined intervention, so $Y_i = Y_i(d)$ when $D_i = d$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No interference fails} if members pass coupon codes to friends, or a discount at one store draws customers from the next.
  \item \textbf{Consistency fails} if ``the coupon'' is sometimes \textyen 10 off and sometimes a free pastry: the effect becomes a mixture over versions.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

As a working rule, state an intervention precise enough that you could, in principle, assign it.

\medskip

\small ``Would the outcome have differed if this person had been perceived as white?'' has no intervention that holds everything else fixed. Audit studies randomize a \emph{signal} instead: Bertrand and Mullainathan (2004) assigned names at random to comparable r\'esum\'es. The estimand is the effect of the signal on the reader's decision.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Pilot: How the 500 Were Chosen}

Marketing targeted the 500 most loyal members, so sure things are overrepresented among coupon holders.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccr}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Coupon (500)} & \textbf{No coupon (500)} & \textbf{Total} \\
\midrule
Lost cause (0,0) & 100 & 300 & 400 \\
Sure thing (1,1) & \textcolor{warnred}{220} & 80 & 300 \\
Persuadable (0,1) & 120 & 80 & 200 \\
Do-not-disturb (1,0) & 60 & 40 & 100 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{align*}
\text{Purchase rate (coupon)} &= (220 + 120)/500 = 0.68 \\
\text{Purchase rate (no coupon)} &= (80 + 40)/500 = 0.24 \\
\text{Naive difference} &= 0.68 - 0.24 = \textcolor{warnred}{0.44}
\end{align*}

The naive difference is \textbf{4.4 times the true effect} of $0.10$. Sure things inflate the coupon group's purchase rate.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Naive Comparison}

\begin{result}{1.6}{Selection-bias decomposition}
Under consistency,
\[
  \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] = \underbrace{\ATT}_{\text{effect on the treated}}
  + \underbrace{\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]}_{\text{selection bias}} .
\]
\end{result}

The proof adds and subtracts $\E[Y(0) \mid D = 1]$, the one number the data never show.

\bigskip

Selection bias asks how coupon holders and the others would have differed \emph{without} the coupon.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Coupons to customers who would buy anyway make it positive.
  \item Coupons to struggling customers make it negative, and can reverse the sign.
  \item It does not shrink as the sample grows.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Pilot, Decomposed}

Read the decomposition off the pilot's type counts. $Y(0) = 1$ for sure things (ST) and do-not-disturbs (DND).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Coupon (500)} & \textbf{No coupon (500)} \\
\midrule
Would buy \emph{without} the coupon: ST $+$ DND & $220 + 60 = 280$ & $80 + 40 = 120$ \\
$\E[Y(0) \mid D]$ & $0.56$ & $0.24$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{align*}
\ATT &= \frac{\#\text{persuadable} - \#\text{do-not-disturb}}{500} = \frac{120 - 60}{500} = 0.12 \\
\text{selection bias} &= 0.56 - 0.24 = 0.32 \\
\text{naive} &= 0.12 + 0.32 = \textcolor{warnred}{0.44}
\end{align*}

Nearly three quarters of marketing's lift is \textbf{who they picked}, not what the coupon did. Even for the pilot's own recipients, $0.12 < 0.227$:
\[
0.12 \times 30 - 6.80 = \textcolor{warnred}{-\text{\textyen}3.20 \text{ per coupon}}.
\]
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Two Kinds of Selection}

\begin{result}{1.7}{Two sources of bias relative to the ATE}
Under consistency,
\[
\begin{aligned}
  \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0] - \ATE &= \underbrace{\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0]}_{\text{baseline selection}}\\
  &\quad + \underbrace{(1-\pi)(\ATT - \ATU)}_{\text{selection on gains}} .
\end{aligned}
\]
\end{result}

\small In the pilot, $\ATU = (80 - 40)/500 = 0.08$, and $0.5 \times 0.12 + 0.5 \times 0.08 = 0.10 = \ATE$ (Result 1.2).

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Source} & \textbf{Size} & \textbf{What marketing did} \\
\midrule
Baseline selection & $0.56 - 0.24 = 0.32$ & picked people who buy anyway \\
Selection on gains & $0.5 \times (0.12 - 0.08) = 0.02$ & picked slightly better responders \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\small Together, $0.32 + 0.02 = 0.34 = 0.44 - 0.10$. A larger pilot would estimate $0.44$ more precisely. \textbf{It would not move it toward $0.10$.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Kinds of Customer}

Lena's customer database splits the whole base into two segments of 5{,}000. Take the segment effects as given for now; estimating effects like these from data comes later in the course.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Segment} & $\E[Y(1) \mid X]$ & $\E[Y(0) \mid X]$ & \textbf{Effect} \\
\midrule
Active ($X = 1$) & 0.80 & 0.75 & $+0.05$ \\
Lapsed ($X = 0$) & 0.30 & 0.10 & $+0.20$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\[
\ATE = 0.5 \times 0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.20 = \mathbf{0.125}
\]

The coupon works four times better on customers who were drifting away. Active customers buy anyway.

\bigskip

\small This is a different population from the pilot region, and it has a different ATE. Both are real answers; neither is \emph{the} effect. That is why step 1 names the population.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Who Got the Coupon Decides the Sign}

Two ways a manager might have run the pilot on the whole base. Treated $=$ one segment, control $=$ the other.

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{(a) Coupon to the active}
\begin{align*}
\E[Y \mid D = 1] &= 0.80 \\
\E[Y \mid D = 0] &= 0.10 \\
\text{naive} &= \textcolor{warnred}{+0.70}
\end{align*}
\[
\underbrace{0.70}_{\text{naive}} = \underbrace{0.05}_{\ATT} + \underbrace{0.75 - 0.10}_{\text{bias} \,=\, +0.65}
\]
\small Active customers buy anyway: a 14-fold overestimate.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.49\textwidth}
\textbf{(b) Coupon to the lapsed}
\begin{align*}
\E[Y \mid D = 1] &= 0.30 \\
\E[Y \mid D = 0] &= 0.75 \\
\text{naive} &= \textcolor{warnred}{-0.45}
\end{align*}
\[
\underbrace{-0.45}_{\text{naive}} = \underbrace{0.20}_{\ATT} + \underbrace{0.10 - 0.75}_{\text{bias} \,=\, -0.65}
\]
\small The coupon looks harmful, yet it lifts the lapsed by 20 points. The sign is reversed.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\medskip

\begin{center}
Same coupon, same customers. The sign is set by \textbf{who was chosen}. Keep (b) in mind. It comes back in Block B, and again later in the course with observational data.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Coin Removes Both}

\begin{assumption}{1.8}{Random assignment}
$D \indep \{Y(0), Y(1)\}$ and $0 < \Pr(D=1) < 1$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{result}{1.9}{Identification by randomization}
Under SUTVA and random assignment, $\ATE = \E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0]$, and $\ATE = \ATT = \ATU$.
\end{result}

\medskip

By consistency, $\E[Y \mid D=d] = \E[Y(d) \mid D=d]$. Independence replaces this with $\E[Y(d)]$: who got the coupon carries no information about potential outcomes. Both bias terms in Result 1.7 are zero.

\medskip

Independence is a property of the procedure, not the customers. A random number generator knows nothing about anyone's potential outcomes, observed or not, so a coin also removes confounding by variables nobody recorded.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Q2 Test: A Coin in the Pilot Region}

In Q2, Lena draws 500 of the same 1{,}000 members at random for the coupon. If the draw split every type exactly in half:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccr}
\toprule

\section{Block B: Causal graphs}

\begin{frame}{A Graph Writes Down Assumptions}

A \textbf{directed acyclic graph} (DAG) states what causes what.

\begin{columns}[c]
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
  \item An arrow $A \to B$ says $A$ may directly cause $B$.
  \item A \emph{missing} arrow says it does not. Missing arrows are the strong claims.
  \item Acyclic: no variable causes itself through a loop.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (x) at (0,1.2) {loyalty};
  \node (d) at (-1.6,0) {coupon};
  \node (y) at (1.6,0) {purchase};
  \draw[->] (x) -- (d); \draw[->] (x) -- (y);
  \draw[->, thick, sbgreen] (d) -- (y);
\end{tikzpicture}

\scriptsize Marketing's pilot, as a graph
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\bigskip

The graph is a claim about the world, not something estimated from the data. Two analysts with different graphs can reach different conclusions from the same table, and the graph shows exactly where they disagree.

\bigskip

Three building blocks decide whether two variables are associated.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Chain, Fork, Collider}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (a1) at (0,0) {$A$}; \node (m1) at (1.3,0) {$M$}; \node (b1) at (2.6,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (a1) -- (m1); \draw[->] (m1) -- (b1); \node at (1.3,-1.0) {\textbf{Chain}};
  \node (a2) at (4.6,0) {$A$}; \node (f2) at (5.9,0.8) {$F$}; \node (b2) at (7.2,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (f2) -- (a2); \draw[->] (f2) -- (b2); \node at (5.9,-1.0) {\textbf{Fork}};
  \node (a3) at (9.2,0) {$A$}; \node (c3) at (10.5,-0.6) {$C$}; \node (b3) at (11.8,0) {$B$};
  \draw[->] (a3) -- (c3); \draw[->] (b3) -- (c3); \node at (10.5,-1.2) {\textbf{Collider}};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llcl}
\toprule
\textbf{Structure} & \textbf{Middle node} & \textbf{$A$, $B$ associated?} & \textbf{Condition on the middle node} \\
\midrule
Chain $A \to M \to B$ & mediator & yes & blocks the association \\
Fork $A \leftarrow F \to B$ & common cause & yes & blocks it \\
Collider $A \to C \leftarrow B$ & common effect & \textbf{no} & \textcolor{warnred}{\textbf{creates} an association} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

In a fork, $A$ and $B$ are associated although neither causes the other. For a collider, conditioning on anything $C$ causes has the same effect as conditioning on $C$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{When Is a Path Open?}

A \textbf{path} is any sequence of arrows between two variables, whatever their direction.

\bigskip

A path is \textbf{blocked} by
\begin{itemize}
  \item a middle node of a chain or fork that we condition on, or
  \item a collider that we do \emph{not} condition on (nor on any of its descendants).
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Two variables are associated, given a conditioning set, only through unblocked paths.

\bigskip

\small Conditioning means comparing within levels of a variable: a control in a regression, a filter on the data, or a sample restricted to one group.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Collider Bias in the Simplest Case}

\begin{result}{1.10}{Collider bias in the simplest case}
Let $D$ and $U$ be independent with $\Pr(D=1) = \Pr(U=1) = \tfrac12$, let $Y = U$ (so $D$ has no effect), and let $C = \max(D, U)$. Then
\[
\E[Y \mid D=1, C=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0, C=1] = \tfrac12 - 1 = -\tfrac12 .
\]
\end{result}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{cccc}
\toprule
$D$ & $U = Y$ & $C$ & kept if $C=1$ \\
\midrule
0 & 0 & 0 & no \\
0 & 1 & 1 & yes \\
1 & 0 & 1 & yes \\
1 & 1 & 1 & yes \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
Four equally likely cells. Among units with $C = 1$, learning $D = 0$ tells you $U = 1$.

\medskip

$D$ has no effect on $Y$, yet the comparison within $C = 1$ finds one.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Backdoor Paths}

A \textbf{backdoor path} from $D$ to $Y$ is a path that starts with an arrow \emph{into} $D$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}]
  \node (x) at (0,1.3) {segment (active or lapsed)};
  \node (d) at (-2.8,0) {coupon $D$};
  \node (y) at (2.8,0) {purchase $Y$};
  \node (a) at (0,-1.2) {app opens in Q2};
  \node (u) at (4.6,-1.2) {plans to visit};
  \draw[->] (x) -- (d); \draw[->] (x) -- (y);
  \draw[->, thick, sbgreen] (d) -- (y);
  \draw[->] (d) -- (a); \draw[->] (u) -- (a); \draw[->] (u) -- (y);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item $D \to Y$ carries the effect of the coupon. Leave it open.
  \item $D \leftarrow \text{segment} \to Y$ is a backdoor path: an association that is not an effect. Block it by comparing within segments.
  \item $D \to \text{app opens} \leftarrow \text{plans to visit} \to Y$ is blocked at the collider, as long as we leave app opens alone.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Adjustment for a Backdoor Set}

\begin{result}{1.11}{Adjustment for a backdoor set}
Suppose a set $X$ of variables (i) contains nothing caused by $D$ and (ii) blocks every backdoor path from $D$ to $Y$. Then conditional exchangeability holds, $\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \indep D \mid X$, and if $0 < \Pr(D=1 \mid X) < 1$,
\[
  \E[Y(d)] = \sum_x \E[Y \mid D=d, X=x]\,\Pr(X=x) .
\]
\end{result}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Condition (ii) closes the non-causal paths. Condition (i) keeps the causal paths open and avoids opening a collider.
  \item Within a level of $X$, coupon holders and the others are comparable, as if a coin had been tossed within each level.
  \item $0 < \Pr(D=1 \mid X) < 1$: every level of $X$ needs both. In design (b) every lapsed customer got the coupon and no active one did, so there is nothing to compare within segments.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Standardization, and the Coin as Graph Surgery}

The formula in Result 1.11 is \textbf{standardization}: compare within levels of $X$, then average with the population's weights. Using the treated units' weights instead gives the ATT.

\bigskip

\begin{frame}{Simpson's Paradox}

An association can reverse when the data are split by a third variable. Which answer is right is decided by the graph, not by the data.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{The third variable is} & \textbf{Graph} & \textbf{Right comparison} \\
\midrule
a fork (common cause of $D$ and $Y$) & $D \leftarrow F \to Y$ & within its levels, then standardize \\
a mediator & $D \to M \to Y$ & pooled; splitting is wrong \\
a collider & $D \to C \leftarrow Y$ & pooled; splitting is wrong \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

The same table of numbers can come from any of the three graphs. Knowing which one you are in is knowledge about the business, not about the data.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimation: The Estimand, Then the Estimator}

\spinestep{Estimand.} The average effect for the 1{,}000 customers in the test region, $\tau_S = \frac{1}{1000}\sum_{i} \tau_i$. These are 2{,}000 fixed potential outcomes. \textbf{The only thing random is the coin.}

\medskip

\spinestep{Estimator.} $\hatt = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$, coupon arm minus control arm.

\medskip

\begin{result}{1.12}{Unbiasedness over the design}
$\hatt = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$ satisfies $\E_D[\hatt] = \tau_S$, where $\E_D$ averages over draws.
\end{result}

\small Each customer lands in the coupon arm with probability $500/1000$, so $\E_D[\bar Y_1] = \frac{1}{1000}\sum_i Y_i(1)$; the same holds for the control arm. The argument needs no outcome model, no sampling assumption, and no large-sample approximation.

\normalsize
\medskip

This is the \textbf{design-based} view: potential outcomes fixed, assignment random.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Random Draw Need Not Balance}

Here is another draw the seed could have produced for the same 1{,}000 customers.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Coupon (500)} & \textbf{No coupon (500)} \\
\midrule
Lost cause & 195 & 205 \\
Sure thing & 155 & 145 \\
Persuadable & 105 & 95 \\
Do-not-disturb & 45 & 55 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\[
\hatt = \frac{155 + 105}{500} - \frac{145 + 55}{500} = 0.52 - 0.40 = \mathbf{0.12} \qquad \text{(true: } 0.10\text{)}
\]

\textbf{Nothing is broken.} Unbiasedness is a statement about all the draws the seed could make, not about this one. The collection of $\hatt$ over every possible draw is the \textbf{randomization distribution}: its center is $\tau_S$, and its spread is what a standard error estimates.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Regression Computes the Same Number}

\begin{result}{1.13}{OLS on a dummy is the difference in means}
In the least-squares fit of $Y_i = \alpha + \beta D_i + \varepsilon_i$, \quad $\hat\alpha = \bar Y_0$ and $\hat\beta = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Data} & $\hat\beta$ & \textbf{Causal?} \\
\midrule
Marketing's pilot & $0.44$ & \textcolor{warnred}{no}: the assignment was a choice \\
Q2 randomized test & $0.10$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes}: the assignment was a coin \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

The regression and the software are the same in both rows, and the verdicts are opposite. The omitted-variable-bias formula is Result 1.6 written for a regression: for binary $D$, $\Cov(D,Y)/\Var(D) = \E[Y(0) \mid D=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid D=0] + \ATT$.

\medskip

\textbf{The regression computes. The design decides whether what it computes is causal.}
\end{frame}

\tau^{\ast} = \frac{0.50}{3} = 0.167 \qquad\qquad \hatt = 0.50 - 0.40 = 0.10

\begin{frame}{Identification, Estimation, Inference}

Keep the three questions apart.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{2.8cm}p{9.8cm}}
\toprule
\spinestep{Identification} & With infinite data, would the comparison equal the estimand? Settled by assumptions about how the data were generated. \\[0.5em]
\spinestep{Estimation} & How is the comparison computed from finite data? \\[0.5em]
\spinestep{Inference} & How far is the estimate likely to be from the truth because of chance? \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

A sophisticated estimator cannot rescue a failed identification argument, and a small standard error does not make a biased comparison right. Marketing's $0.44$ failed at the first question; no amount of data would fix it at the third.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Three Common Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{three common errors}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Holding a lift against zero instead of the break-even of the population the decision covers.
  \item ``Controlling for everything available'': a mediator removes part of the effect and a collider adds bias.
  \item Reading a large sample as protection against selection bias. It only makes the wrong number more precise.
\end{itemize}
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one has appeared today: the Q2 lift of $0.10$ is positive but below its break-even of $0.167$; \texttt{app\_opens\_q2} looks like a sensible filter and is a collider; and a bigger pilot would only have pinned down the wrong $0.44$.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} Name the number the decision needs, for whom, before any data. Here it is a lift held against $\tau^\ast = p_1/3$.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} Naive $=$ ATT $+$ selection bias; who got the coupon can inflate an effect or reverse its sign. A coin removes the bias by procedure. Without one, a graph says what to compare within: block backdoor paths, and never condition on a mediator or a collider.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} The difference in means, the same number as OLS on a dummy, is unbiased over draws of the coin. Any one draw misses.
  \item \textbf{An effect can be real and unprofitable.} The expensive customer is the sure thing.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
