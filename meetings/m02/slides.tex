\section{Block A: Experiments I, estimation}

% [RELEASE R1: the Q2 result and the two readings of it]
\begin{frame}{The Q2 Test, Read Two Ways}

In the 1{,}000-member test region, 500 members were drawn at random for the \textyen 10 coupon.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Members} & \textbf{Bought} & \textbf{Purchase rate} \\
\midrule
Coupon & 500 & 250 & 0.50 \\
No coupon & 500 & 200 & 0.40 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Marketing: \emph{``It was randomized, and it's significant. The coupon works. Roll it out.''}

\medskip

The CFO: \emph{``Last week you said it loses \textyen 2 a coupon. Which is it?''}

\bigskip

Both can be right. They ask different questions, and each answer comes from \textbf{one draw of the coin}. Today you will see how far one draw can mislead, which question the decision needs, and how to design the next test.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Number That Decides It}

From Meeting 1: \textyen 30 margin, a \textyen 10 coupon paid on every holder's purchase, so $\tau^\ast = c\,p_1/m = p_1/3$.

\begin{center}
\Large $\tau^{\ast} = 0.50/3 = \mathbf{0.167}$
\end{center}

Two different null hypotheses, two different decisions:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Null} & \textbf{Who asks} \\
\midrule
Does the coupon do anything? & $\tau = 0$ & marketing \\
Does the coupon pay? & $\tau \leq 0.167$ & the CFO \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Every estimate today comes with an interval. The question is \textbf{where the interval sits relative to $0$ and to $0.167$}.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Exactly Are We Uncertain About?}

\spinestep{Estimand.} $\tau_S = \frac{1}{1000}\sum_i \{Y_i(1) - Y_i(0)\}$ for the 1{,}000 members of the region: \textbf{a fixed number}. Meeting 1 showed the draw makes $\hatt = \bar Y_1 - \bar Y_0$ unbiased for it.

\bigskip

So where does the uncertainty come from? The two standard frames give different answers.

\medskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{p{3.2cm}p{4.6cm}p{4.6cm}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Design-based} (first) & \textbf{Superpopulation} \\
\midrule
Customers & fixed: these 1{,}000 & an i.i.d.\ sample from a larger population \\
Random & only the coin & the coin \emph{and} the sample \\
Estimand & $\tau_S$, this region & the population's ATE \\
Needs & nothing but the draw log & a sampling assumption \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Analyze as Drawn}

Marketing proposes a ``cleaner'' comparison: drop members who never opened the app during the test, \emph{``they never saw the coupon''}.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Opening the app is measured after the draw, and the coupon's notification makes it more likely. It is a post-treatment variable.
    \item Dropping non-openers keeps different people in the two arms. The coin no longer decides who is compared.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\begin{block}{Compare the arms as the coin made them}
The difference over \emph{all} drawn members is the effect of \emph{assignment}, the \textbf{intention-to-treat (ITT) effect}. It is also the number the decision needs, because the chain controls sending, not reading.
\end{block}

\medskip

\small The effect of \emph{seeing} the coupon is a different estimand, with its own assumptions. We come back to it at the end of the meeting.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Check the Draw Before Reading the Outcome}

\textbf{Sample-ratio mismatch.} Compare the realized arm sizes with the planned allocation:
\[
\chi^2 = \sum_d \frac{(n_d - \E n_d)^2}{\E n_d} \quad \text{(one degree of freedom)}.
\]

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Coupon} & \textbf{No coupon} & $\chi^2$ & $p$ \\
\midrule
Q2 test, planned 500 / 500 & 500 & 500 & 0 & 1 \\
A broken log, planned 500 / 500 & 560 & 440 & 14.4 & 0.0001 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A tiny $p$-value (in practice below about $0.001$) means the draw or the logging is broken: a filter that removed more members from one arm, a delivery failure. The arms are no longer a random split, and \textbf{no outcome analysis proceeds} until the cause is found.

\bigskip

\small Two more threats to the draw come later today: one member's outcome depending on another's coupon (block B), and members who never see their coupon (the end of block B).
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Fisher: Could the Draw Alone Explain It?}

\begin{result}{1.1}{Randomization test of the sharp null}
Under the sharp null $Y_i(1) = Y_i(0)$ for every $i$, the observed outcomes are both potential outcomes, so the distribution of any statistic over the $\binom{n}{n_1}$ equally likely draws is known exactly. The $p$-value is the share of draws giving a statistic at least as extreme as the observed one.
\end{result}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Tea (Fisher 1935).} Eight cups, four with the milk poured first. If she is guessing, all $\binom{8}{4} = 70$ labelings are equally likely, so a perfect score has probability $1/70 = 0.014$.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{Coupon.} Under the sharp null, 450 buyers and 550 non-buyers whatever the coin said. Over all draws, with the pooled rate $0.45$,
$\SE_0 = \sqrt{2(0.45)(0.55)/500} = 0.0315$, so $z = 0.10/0.0315 = 3.18$, $p \approx 0.0015$.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\medskip

The test needs no model and no sampling assumption, only the draw. Its limitation is its null: ``no effect for \emph{anyone}'', not ``zero on average'' or ``does not pay''.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Neyman: How Far Does One Draw Land?}

\begin{result}{1.2}{Neyman's variance}
Holding the potential outcomes fixed,
\[
  \Var_D(\hatt) = \frac{S_1^2}{n_1} + \frac{S_0^2}{n_0} - \frac{S_\tau^2}{n}.
\]
\end{result}

\begin{result}{1.3}{The usual variance estimator is conservative}
With $s_d^2$ the sample variance in arm $d$, $\widehat\Var = s_1^2/n_1 + s_0^2/n_0$ satisfies $\E_D[\widehat\Var] = S_1^2/n_1 + S_0^2/n_0 \ge \Var_D(\hatt)$, with equality if and only if every unit has the same effect.
\end{result}

The last term needs \textbf{both potential outcomes of each member}, which no experiment reveals. Dropping it is the price of not knowing individual effects.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{How Conservative, on Meeting 1's Region}

The pilot region's types (400 lost causes, 300 sure things, 200 persuadables, 100 do-not-disturbs), split 500/500:
\[
S_1^2 = \tfrac{250}{999}, \quad S_0^2 = \tfrac{240}{999}, \quad S_\tau^2 = \tfrac{290}{999} .
\]
\textbf{True SD over draws $= 0.026$; the usual SE $= 0.031$.}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[width=0.72\textwidth, height=4.2cm, domain=0:0.2, samples=120, xlabel={$\hatt$ over draws},
  ytick=\empty, axis lines=left, legend style={draw=none, font=\scriptsize, at={(0.99,0.97)}, anchor=north east},
  xtick={0,0.05,0.10,0.15,0.20}, xticklabel style={/pgf/number format/fixed, font=\scriptsize}]
\addplot[thick, sbgreen] {exp(-(x-0.10)^2/(2*0.026^2))/(0.026*sqrt(2*pi))};
\addplot[thick, dashed, noteblue] {exp(-(x-0.10)^2/(2*0.031^2))/(0.031*sqrt(2*pi))};
\legend{true randomization distribution (SD $0.026$), normal with the usual SE ($0.031$)}
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\small The gap is effect heterogeneity we cannot see. The usual SE describes a wider distribution than the one the draw produces.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Same Formula, Read for a Population}

\begin{result}{1.4}{Superpopulation variance}
If units are i.i.d.\ draws and assignment is independent of potential outcomes, $\Var(\hatt) = \sigma_1^2/n_1 + \sigma_0^2/n_0$, estimated by the same $\widehat\Var$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

On the Q2 test:
\[
\widehat\SE = \sqrt{\frac{0.50 \times 0.50}{500} + \frac{0.40 \times 0.60}{500}} = \sqrt{\frac{0.25}{500} + \frac{0.24}{500}} = \mathbf{0.0313}.
\]

Two readings of one number: a conservative SE for these 1{,}000 members, or the exact SE for a population they were sampled from.

\bigskip

\textbf{In a regression.} Meeting 1's OLS on a dummy gives the same $\hatt$. With heteroskedasticity-robust (HC2) standard errors it also gives this SE; the default homoskedastic SE is wrong when the arms differ in size and variance.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Testing and the Confidence Interval}

Against zero, with the SE above:
\[
z = \frac{\hatt}{\widehat\SE} = \frac{0.10}{0.0313} = 3.19, \qquad p \approx 0.0014 .
\]

95\% confidence interval:
\[
\hatt \pm 1.96\,\widehat\SE = 0.10 \pm 0.061 = [0.039,\; 0.161] .
\]

\bigskip

\textbf{What the 95\% means.} Across draws of the coin, at least about 95\% of intervals built this way contain $\tau_S$ (``at least'', because the SE is conservative). It is not a 95\% probability that this interval contains a fixed number.

\bigskip

The interval is the set of null values the test does not reject. Zero is one point in it, with nothing special about it unless the decision is about zero.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Test Against the Break-Even, Not Against Zero}

Same estimate, same $\widehat\SE$, the CFO's null:
\[
H_0 : \tau \leq \tau^\ast = 0.1667 \;\; \text{(does not pay)}, \qquad
z = \frac{0.100 - 0.1667}{0.0313} = -2.13 .
\]

The estimate is \textbf{two standard errors below} break-even, and the interval $[0.039,\, 0.161]$ excludes \emph{both} $0$ and $0.167$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Where the interval sits} & \textbf{Decision} \\
\midrule
Entirely above $\tau^\ast$ & Roll out \\
Straddles $\tau^\ast$ & Undecided on profit: a bigger or sharper test \\
Between $0$ and $\tau^\ast$ & \textcolor{warnred}{It works and it does not pay} \quad $\leftarrow$ Q2 \\
Includes $0$ & Cannot yet tell it works \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

``Significant'' answered marketing's question. It was never the decision.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Put the Interval on the Quantity the Decision Is Made On}

The break-even used $\hat p_1 = 0.50$, itself an estimate. Write the decision variable as a combination of arm means: $V = m\tau - c\,\mu_1 = (m - c)\mu_1 - m\mu_0$.

\begin{result}{1.5}{Inference on net value}
$\hat V = (m-c)\bar Y_1 - m\bar Y_0$ is unbiased for $V$ over the draw, with (conservative) variance estimate $(m-c)^2 s_1^2/n_1 + m^2 s_0^2/n_0$.
\end{result}

\[
\hat V = 20(0.50) - 30(0.40) = -\text{\textyen}2.00
\]
\[
\widehat\Var = 400 \times 0.0005 + 900 \times 0.00048 = 0.632, \qquad \widehat\SE = 0.795 .
\]

\begin{center}
\textbf{Net per coupon $-$\textyen 2.00, 95\% CI $[-$\textyen 3.56$,\; -$\textyen 0.44$]$.}
\end{center}

The whole interval loses money. Marketing and the CFO were both right; only the CFO asked the decision's question.
\end{frame}

Two regions propose Q3 campaigns, each tested properly with a 50/50 draw \textbf{within its own list}. The segment effects from Meeting 1 are $+0.05$ for active and $+0.20$ for lapsed members.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}

\textbf{List} & \textbf{Active} & \textbf{Lapsed} & \textbf{Effect the test measures} \\

\bigskip

Both tests are internally valid. Each measures its own list's effect, and neither is ``the'' effect of the coupon.

\medskip

The campaign brief says \emph{``The same coupon, in the same app, in the same quarter.''} That sentence is the whole case for carrying segment effects from one list to another.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Transport to Another Population}

\begin{assumption}{1.6}{Conditional effect invariance}
$\tau_T(x) = \tau_S(x)$ for every $x$ in $T$'s support, and that support is contained in $S$'s.
\end{assumption}

\begin{result}{1.7}{Transport by reweighting}
Under Assumption 1.6, $\tau_T = \sum_x \Pr_T(X=x)\,\tau_S(x)$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The brief's sentence is the argument for Assumption 1.6: same coupon, same channel, same season, so segment effects carry over.
  \item It fails when the effect depends on something not in $X$ that differs between populations: the season, the channel, the price level.
  \item Types absent from the experiment cannot be reweighted into it.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Analyze the arms as drawn; check the arm sizes before any outcome.
  \item Fisher asks whether the draw alone could explain the gap. Neyman says how far one draw lands; the usual SE is conservative for a fixed population (0.031 against a true 0.026).
  \item Test against the break-even, or better, put the interval on net value: Q2 works and does not pay, $-$\textyen 2.00 [$-$3.56, $-$0.44].
  \item Another population is another estimand, reached only by an assumption; move the break-even with it.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{The Next Test}

The Q2 test settled one question (do not coupon everyone). Lena proposes two Q3 tests:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A lapsed-only coupon test, to learn whether the coupon pays on lapsed members. The planning values are $p_1 = 0.30$ and $p_0 = 0.10$, a lift of $0.20$ against a break-even of $0.10$.
    \item An in-store buy-one-get-one (BOGO) offer. It runs on the menu board, so it cannot be sent to individuals.
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip

The constraint is the test window. It reaches about 350 lapsed members, \textbf{175 per arm}.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
The test must tell $0.20$ from $0.10$: its \textbf{minimum detectable effect} (MDE) must be at most the gap to break-even, $0.10$.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fix It in Writing Before the First Coupon Goes Out}

\begin{center}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Sample Size and the Minimum Detectable Effect}

\begin{result}{1.8}{Sample size}
With $n$ units per arm, a level-$\alpha$ two-sided test has approximate power $1-\beta$ against an effect $\delta$ away from the null value when
\[
  n = \frac{(z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})^2(\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2)}{\delta^2}, \quad\text{rounded up.}
\]
For a one-sided test replace $z_{1-\alpha/2}$ by $z_{1-\alpha}$.
\end{result}

Inverted: $\text{MDE} = (z_{1-\alpha/2} + z_{1-\beta})\SE$, about $1.96 + 0.84 = 2.80$ SEs at 5\% and 80\% power.

\medskip

\textbf{Check on Q2.} To detect $0.10$ with $\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2 = 0.25 + 0.24 = 0.49$: $n = 2.80^2 \times 0.49/0.01 = 384.2$, so \textbf{385} per arm. Q2 had 500, so its MDE was $2.80 \times 0.0313 = 0.088$.

\medskip

\small Q2 was built to tell $0.10$ from zero. The CFO's gap is $0.167 - 0.10 = 0.067$, below that MDE: the test was underpowered for the profit question and separated them on this draw anyway.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Lapsed Test Is Too Small}

Planning values for lapsed members: $p_1 = 0.30$, $p_0 = 0.10$.
\[
\sigma_1^2 + \sigma_0^2 = 0.30(0.70) + 0.10(0.90) = 0.21 + 0.09 = 0.30
\]

Needed for an MDE of $0.10$:
\[
n = \frac{7.84 \times 0.30}{0.01} = 235.2 \;\Rightarrow\; \textcolor{warnred}{\mathbf{236} \text{ per arm}} \quad > \quad 175 \text{ available.}
\]

Available at 175 per arm:
\[
\text{MDE} = 2.80\sqrt{0.30/175} = 2.80 \times 0.0414 = \textcolor{warnred}{0.116} \quad > \quad 0.10 .
\]

\bigskip

Two ways out: wait a quarter for more lapsed members, or \textbf{make each member's observation carry more information}. Halving an MDE takes four times the sample; the second way costs nothing.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Variance Reduction With a Pre-Treatment Covariate}

Let $X$ be fixed before the draw (Q1 purchases) and analyze $Y^{\text{cv}} = Y - \theta(X - \bar X)$.

\begin{result}{1.9}{CUPED}
For any fixed $\theta$, $\hatt^{\text{cv}} = \hatt - \theta(\bar X_1 - \bar X_0)$ is unbiased over the draw. Within an arm, $\Var(Y - \theta X)$ is minimized at $\theta^\ast = \Cov(Y,X)/\Var(X)$, where it equals $\Var(Y)(1-\rho^2)$, $\rho = \mathrm{Corr}(Y, X)$.
\end{result}

\begin{itemize}
  \item In any one draw $\bar X_1 \ne \bar X_0$, so the adjusted estimate moves: that shift corrects chance imbalance.
  \item CUPED is regression adjustment: $Y = \alpha + \tau D + \beta X + \varepsilon$. Adding $D \times (X - \bar X)$, as in Lin (2013), guarantees it never hurts in large samples.
  \item \textbf{Precision, not identification.} The draw identified the effect. If $X$ were affected by treatment, the correction would subtract part of the effect.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{CUPED on the Lapsed Test}

\begin{frame}{Whose Outcome Depends on Whose Treatment?}

SUTVA, from Meeting 1, asked for \textbf{no interference}: unit $i$'s outcome depends only on its own treatment. Without it, the difference in means compares two mixtures of spillovers, not ``everyone treated'' with ``no one treated''.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{6.2cm}p{6.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{What the chain says} & \textbf{What it means for the unit} \\
\midrule
``Q2 codes were single-use and tied to the account.'' & No interference between members: the member is a valid unit. \\[0.4em]
``The BOGO goes on the menu board; everyone in the store sees it.'' & Customers in one store share one treatment: the unit is the \textbf{store}. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

This is a failure of identification, and no variance formula repairs it. The remedy is a unit of assignment large enough to contain the spillover.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Design Effect}

\begin{result}{1.10}{Design effect}
For a cluster of $m$ units with common variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho_c$, $\Var(\text{cluster mean}) = \tfrac{\sigma^2}{m}[1 + (m-1)\rho_c]$: the variance is inflated by $\text{DEFF} = 1 + (m-1)\rho_c$ relative to $m$ independent units.
\end{result}

\textbf{The BOGO test:} 50 stores (25 per arm), about 400 customers per store-week, 20{,}000 customers in all. In the Q2 data the within-store correlation of weekly purchase is $\rho_c = 0.01$:
\[
\text{DEFF} = 1 + 399 \times 0.01 = 4.99, \qquad \sqrt{4.99} = 2.23, \qquad 20{,}000/4.99 \approx 4{,}000 .
\]

A correlation of $0.01$ makes the correct SE 2.2 times the customer-level one: 20{,}000 customers carry the information of about 4{,}000. The coin was tossed 50 times, not 20{,}000.

\medskip

\small Compare store means, or cluster the SE by store. With fewer than about 40 clusters, use small-sample corrections or randomization inference over store assignments.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Peeking and Many Metrics}

The 5\% error rate holds for \textbf{one look} at a sample size fixed in advance.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Stopping at the first $p < 0.05$ over ten equally spaced looks (peeking) raises the false-positive rate under no effect to about 19\%. Look once, or use a method built for monitoring (group-sequential boundaries, always-valid tests).
  \item Twenty metrics at 5\% yield about one false positive when nothing works. Declare one primary metric tied to the decision; treat the others as guardrails or exploration.
  \item When several tests drive one decision, control the family-wise error with Holm's step-down procedure: order the $p$-values, compare the $k$-th smallest with $\alpha/(K - k + 1)$, and stop at the first failure.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\small For the lapsed test, the stopping rule is ``analyze once, at the end'', and the primary metric is purchase within 7 days. A daily dashboard is a guardrail, not a decision rule.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Assignment and Receipt}

Write $Z$ for assignment (the draw) and $D$ for receipt (saw the coupon). Each member has potential receipts $D(0), D(1)$ and potential outcomes $Y(d, z)$. The draw gives independence. Two more assumptions:

\begin{assumption}{1.11}{Exclusion}
$Y(d, 0) = Y(d, 1) \equiv Y(d)$: assignment affects the outcome only through receipt.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}{1.12}{Monotonicity}
$D(1) \ge D(0)$ for every unit: nobody takes the treatment because they were \emph{not} offered it.
\end{assumption}

\bigskip

Neither assumption is checkable from the data. Exclusion is argued from the design: here, a notification that does not show the amount. Monotonicity is argued from behavior; here it holds trivially, because nobody in the control arm can see a coupon.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What the Draw Alone Identifies}

\begin{result}{1.13}{Two effects of assignment}
Under random assignment alone, $\text{ITT} \equiv \E[Y \mid Z=1] - \E[Y \mid Z=0] = \E[Y(D(1),1) - Y(D(0),0)]$ and the first stage $\pi \equiv \E[D \mid Z=1] - \E[D \mid Z=0] = \E[D(1) - D(0)]$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

Q2: ITT $= 0.50 - 0.40 = 0.10$; first stage $\pi = 400/500 - 0 = 0.80$.

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The ITT is the effect of the offer through every channel, with take-up as it happened. It is the number a decision to send needs, and it needs neither exclusion nor monotonicity.
  \item Those two are needed only to attribute an effect to \emph{seeing} the coupon.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Compliance Types}

Meeting 1's response types, written for receipt instead of the outcome:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & $D(0)$ & $D(1)$ & \textbf{In the Q2 test} \\
\midrule
Always-taker & 1 & 1 & sees a coupon without one: impossible here \\
Never-taker & 0 & 0 & never opens the app that week \\
Complier & 0 & 1 & sees it if, and only if, sent it \\
Defier & 1 & 0 & ruled out by monotonicity \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{result}{1.14}{Type shares}
Under random assignment and monotonicity, $\pi_A = \Pr(D=1 \mid Z=0)$, $\pi_N = \Pr(D=0 \mid Z=1)$, and $\pi_C = \pi$.
\end{result}

Q2: $\pi_A = 0$, $\pi_N = 100/500 = 0.20$, $\pi_C = 0.80$. As with response types, nobody's compliance type is observed; only shares and averages are.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The LATE}

\begin{result}{1.15}{The LATE theorem (Imbens and Angrist)}
Under random assignment, exclusion, and monotonicity, with $\pi > 0$,
\[
  \frac{\text{ITT}}{\pi} = \frac{\E[Y \mid Z=1] - \E[Y \mid Z=0]}{\E[D \mid Z=1] - \E[D \mid Z=0]} = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid \text{complier}] \equiv \text{LATE}.
\]
\end{result}

\begin{result}{1.16}{One-sided noncompliance}
If no unit assigned to control can receive the treatment ($D(0) = 0$ for all), then under random assignment and exclusion, $\text{ITT}/\pi = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid D = 1]$: the ratio is the effect on those who received it.
\end{result}

Q2: $0.10/0.80 = \mathbf{0.125}$, the effect of seeing the coupon on members who saw it. Always- and never-takers contribute nothing to the ITT; the ratio rescales it to the compliers.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why the Other Two Comparisons Fail}

Coupon-arm non-openers are never-takers, and by exclusion their $20/100 = 0.20$ is an untreated rate. The control arm then gives compliers' untreated rate:
\[
0.40 = 0.80\,\mu_{C0} + 0.20 \times 0.20 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \mu_{C0} = 0.36/0.80 = 0.45, \qquad 0.575 - 0.45 = 0.125 .
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Openers only} ($0.075$, too low). Opening is partly caused by the draw. The 360 control openers are members who open the app anyway, and they buy more without a coupon ($0.50$) than compliers as a whole ($0.45$).
  \item \textbf{Saw vs did not} ($0.208$, too high). It compares compliers with a mix that includes never-takers, who buy at only $0.20$.
\end{itemize}

Both condition on receipt, which assignment caused: Meeting 1's selection bias, created by a post-treatment variable.

\bigskip

\small If the notification had said ``\textyen 10 off'', non-openers would be partly treated and exclusion would fail: the ITT would include a direct effect, and dividing by $0.80$ inflates it.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
    \item \spinestep{Estimand.} The Q2 estimand is the region's average effect, a fixed number. Another list is another estimand, reached by a transport assumption; move the break-even with it.
    \item \spinestep{Identification.} The draw, as in Meeting 1. Analyze as drawn, because ``openers only'' undoes it. With noncompliance the draw still identifies the ITT; the LATE needs exclusion and monotonicity too.
    \item \spinestep{Uncertainty from the design.} Fisher asks whether the draw alone could explain the gap; Neyman says how far one draw lands. The SE lives at the level the coin was tossed.
    \item \textbf{Significant is not profitable.} Put the interval on net value. Q2 works and does not pay.
    \item \textbf{Design for the question.} The MDE is 2.8 SEs and must fit inside the gap to break-even; CUPED buys precision with a pre-period column; fix the plan before the first coupon goes out.
\end{itemize}
