\begin{frame}{The CATE}

With treatment $D$, outcome $Y$, and pre-treatment covariates $X$, write $\mu_d(x) = \E[Y \mid D=d, X=x]$.

\begin{defbox}{conditional average treatment effect (CATE)}
\[
\tau(x) = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X = x].
\]
It is a function; the ATE is its average, $\ATE = \E[\tau(X)]$.
\end{defbox}

Once identified, every outcome regression splits into a \textbf{baseline} and an \textbf{effect}:
\[
\mu_d(x) = \mu_0(x) + d\,\tau(x).
\]

A decision about a target population $T$ (a list, a region) needs $\tau_T = \sum_x \Pr_T(X=x)\,\tau(x)$, which standardizes the CATE with $T$'s weights.

\medskip

\small On the base, half active and half lapsed: $\tau_T = 0.5 \times 0.05 + 0.5 \times 0.20 = 0.125$, the ATE of Meeting 1.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Prognostic Variables and Effect Modifiers}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A variable is \textbf{prognostic} if $\mu_0(x)$ varies with it. It predicts the outcome level.
  \item It is an \textbf{effect modifier} if $\tau(x)$ varies with it. It predicts who responds.
\end{itemize}

The two are independent properties: a variable can be either, both, or neither. Segment is both. Distance to the nearest store might be prognostic only, since nearby members buy more with or without a coupon.

\bigskip

Prognostic variation dominates. Across the base,
\[
\Var\bigl(\mu_0(X)\bigr) = 0.325^2 = 0.106, \qquad \Var\bigl(\tau(X)\bigr) = 0.075^2 = 0.0056 :
\]
about \textbf{nineteen times} as much variation in \emph{who buys} as in \emph{who is moved}.

\medskip

This is typical of business data, and it is why learners tuned to predict outcomes favor prognostic variables. Block B returns to it.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Identification of the CATE}

\begin{assumption}{1.1}{Conditional independence}
$\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \indep D \mid X$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{1.2}{Overlap on the decision population}
$0 < \Pr(D=1 \mid X=x) < 1$ for every $x$ at which a decision will be made.
\end{assumption}

\begin{result}{1.3}{Identification of the CATE}
Under consistency and Assumptions 1.1--1.2, $\tau(x) = \mu_1(x) - \mu_0(x)$.
\end{result}

\medskip

An experiment satisfies conditional independence by design, and overlap for every $x$ \emph{in the experiment}. Values of $x$ the experiment never saw have no identified CATE: any estimate there is the model's extrapolation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Support or Confounding?}

Keep the two failures apart. Four ways a coupon could have been assigned:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{4.2cm}p{2.3cm}p{1.8cm}p{4.3cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Assignment} & \textbf{Indep.\ given $X$} & \textbf{Overlap} & \textbf{What the data identify} \\
\midrule
50/50 draw among loyalty members only & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes} & \textcolor{warnred}{members only} & $\tau(x)$ for members; for non-members, extrapolation \\[0.4em]
Draw at 70\% for gold tier, 30\% for silver & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes} & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes} & $\tau(x)$ for every $x$; the pooled difference in means is biased for the ATE \\[0.4em]
Coupon to every member who complained & trivially yes, given complaint & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & nothing: no complainer untreated, no other member treated \\[0.4em]
A manager picks members ``likely to respond'' & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & may hold & nothing without the manager's information \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

A deterministic rule makes independence given its input automatic and useless: overlap fails, so no comparison is left (a support problem). Assignment on something outside $X$ can leave overlap intact while independence fails (a confounding problem).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{From the CATE to a Targeting Rule}

A treated unit generates margin $m$ per purchase, costs $c$ per purchase made while treated (a redeemed coupon) and $k$ per unit treated regardless (a phone call).

\begin{result}{1.4}{Individual targeting rule}
The expected net value of treating a unit with covariates $x$ is $V(x) = m\,\tau(x) - c\,\mu_1(x) - k$. Without a budget, the value-maximizing rule treats $x$ if and only if $V(x) > 0$, and its value per unit is
\[
\E\big[\max\{V(X), 0\}\big] \;\ge\; \max\{\E[V(X)],\, 0\},
\]
the value of the best blanket rule. With a budget of $B$ treatments, treat the $B$ units with the largest positive $V(x)$.
\end{result}

At Houtan Coffee, $m = 30$, $c = 10$, $k = 0$: \quad coupon member $x$ $\iff$ $\tau(x) > \mu_1(x)/3$.

\smallskip
The rule needs \textbf{two numbers per member}: the lift and the purchase rate with the coupon.
\end{frame}

\bigskip

\textbf{The value of targeting.} Per member of the base:
\begin{itemize}
  \item coupon everyone: $\E[V(X)] = 0.5(-6.50) + 0.5(3.00) = -$\textyen 1.75;
  \item best blanket rule: coupon nobody, \textyen 0;
  \item the rule: $\E[\max\{V(X), 0\}] = 0.5 \times 3.00 = $ \textyen 1.50, which is the \textyen 15{,}000 list.
\end{itemize}
An average below break-even can hide a profitable segment.

\medskip

\textbf{Ranking by ``most likely to buy'' is not the rule}, and here it is its opposite. With a cost per redemption, the bar $c\,\mu_1(x)/m$ \emph{rises} with the purchase rate: likely buyers must clear a higher bar. A response model ranks sure things first; the rule wants persuadables.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Read the Model Through Its Predictions}

Coding sets the sign, not the substance. With $X = 1$ active, $\tau(x) = 0.20 - 0.15x$. Recode $L = 1$ for lapsed: $\tau(x) = 0.05 + 0.15L$. The two effects are unchanged.

\bigskip

The coefficient on $D$ is not an average. $\widehat\tau = 0.20$ is the effect at the reference group ($X = 0$, lapsed). The average for a population $T$ is $\widehat\tau + \widehat\gamma\,\E_T[X]$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Target population} & \textbf{Share active} & \textbf{Average effect} \\
\midrule
The whole base & 0.5 & $0.20 - 0.15 \times 0.5 = 0.125$ \\
A list that is 80\% active & 0.8 & $0.20 - 0.15 \times 0.8 = 0.08$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

A row's $\widehat\tau(x)$ is a group average. A lapsed member's $0.20$ means about 200 extra purchases per 1{,}000 similar members, without knowing \emph{which} 200.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Is the Gap Itself Noise?}

Behind the segment rates: 200 members per arm in each segment of the Q2 test in the pilot region.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Segment} & \textbf{Coupon} & \textbf{No coupon} & \textbf{Effect} & \textbf{SE} \\
\midrule
Active & 160/200 & 150/200 & $0.05$ & $\sqrt{0.16/200 + 0.1875/200} = 0.042$ \\
Lapsed & 60/200 & 20/200 & $0.20$ & $\sqrt{0.21/200 + 0.09/200} = 0.039$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{result}{1.5}{Standard error of a difference between segments}
For segment effects estimated on disjoint randomized samples, $\SE(\hatt_a - \hatt_b) = \sqrt{\SE(\hatt_a)^2 + \SE(\hatt_b)^2}$.
\end{result}

\[
\hatt_L - \hatt_A = 0.15, \qquad \SE = \sqrt{0.042^2 + 0.039^2} = 0.057, \qquad z = 2.64 .
\]

\textbf{Test the gap.} ``Significant for lapsed, not for active'' is not evidence of a difference: active's $0.05$ has interval $[-0.03, 0.13]$, and ``not significant'' is not ``zero''.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Many Segments, One False Positive}

The gap's two-sided $p$-value is $0.008$. Whether that is convincing depends on how many gaps were looked at.

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item With $K$ segments each tested at level $\alpha$, the chance of a false positive somewhere approaches $1 - (1-\alpha)^K$. For the 14 CRM fields at $\alpha = 0.05$: $1 - 0.95^{14} = 0.51$.
  \item If segment was the one pre-specified comparison, $p = 0.008 < 0.05$ stands.
  \item If segment was found by searching the 14 fields, Bonferroni asks for $p < 0.05/14 = 0.0036$, and $0.008$ fails. Holm's first step is the same test.
  \item Report all segments examined. Segments found by searching need confirmation on fresh data.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\textbf{Group effects.} For a grouping $G$, $\text{GATE}_k = \E[\tau(X) \mid G = k]$, estimated by the within-group difference in means. The two segment effects above are GATEs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The CATE $\tau(x)$ is a function; a list's average effect uses that list's weights.
  \item Prognostic variables move the level; effect modifiers move the effect. Here the level varies nineteen times as much.
  \item The draw identifies $\tau(x)$ within every $x$, but only on the test's support.
  \item Coupon $x$ iff $\tau(x) > \mu_1(x)/3$: Lena's list earns $+$\textyen 15{,}000, marketing's loses \textyen 32{,}500.
  \item Test the gap between segments, and count the segments you looked at.
\end{itemize}

\section{Block B: Meta-learners}
% ====================================================================

\begin{frame}{From Two Segments to Fourteen Fields}

With two segments the CATE is two differences in means. The Q2 export has 14 CRM fields: recency, visit frequency, tier, spend, app use, store, and more. There are more cells than members.

\bigskip

\textbf{Meta-learners} turn any prediction model into a CATE estimator:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{9.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Learner} & \textbf{What it fits} \\
\midrule
S & one model of $Y$ on $(X, D)$ \\
T & one model of $Y$ on $X$ per arm \\
X & two arms, then each arm's imputed effects \\
Transformed outcome, DR, R & a regression whose target \emph{is} $\tau(x)$ \\
TARNet & a shared representation of $x$, two heads \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Any supervised learner can fit one of these. The question is what each one risks.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{S-Learner: One Model, $D$ as a Feature}

Fit $\hat f(x, d) \approx \E[Y \mid X = x, D = d]$ with $D$ alongside $X$; then $\hatt(x) = \hat f(x,1) - \hat f(x,0)$. The interaction model is an S-learner fitted by OLS.

\begin{result}{1.6}{Regularization shrinks heterogeneity in the S-learner}
In a balanced design with centered, orthogonal regressors $\tilde X$, $\tilde D = D - \tfrac12$, and $\tilde D\tilde X$, penalizing only the interaction by $\lambda\gamma^2$ gives $\hat\gamma_\lambda = \hat\gamma_{\text{OLS}} \cdot s/(s + \lambda)$ with $s = \sum_i (\tilde D_i \tilde X_i)^2$; the other coefficients are unchanged.
\end{result}

\medskip

Since $\tilde D = \pm\tfrac12$, $s = \tfrac14\sum_i \tilde X_i^2$: the interaction column has \textbf{a quarter of $X$'s variation}. A penalty that barely moves $\beta$ can erase $\gamma$.

\medskip

A lasso sets a small $\gamma$ exactly to zero, and the model reports the same effect for everyone. Trees do the same implicitly: a split on $D$ competes with prognostic splits and loses when the effect is small.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{How Much the Penalty Removes}

The pilot region's segment test has 800 members, 200 per arm per segment. Center both columns: $\tilde D = \pm\tfrac12$, $\tilde X = \pm\tfrac12$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Column's sum of squares} & \textbf{Kept by a penalty $\lambda = 50$} \\
\midrule
$\tilde X$ (segment) & $800 \times \tfrac14 = 200$ & $200/250 = 0.80$ \\
$\tilde D\tilde X$ (interaction) & $800 \times \tfrac1{16} = 50$ & $50/100 = 0.50$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The same penalty keeps 80\% of a prognostic coefficient and half of the interaction:
\[
\hat\gamma: -0.15 \to -0.075, \qquad \hatt(\text{active}) = 0.125 - \tfrac12(0.075) = 0.0875, \qquad \hatt(\text{lapsed}) = 0.1625 .
\]

\medskip

The gap between segments halves, from 0.15 to 0.075. As $\lambda$ grows, both effects go to the average, $0.125$, and the model no longer separates persuadables from sure things.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{T-Learner: Separate the Arms}

Fit $\hat\mu_1$ on treated rows and $\hat\mu_0$ on control rows; $\hatt(x) = \hat\mu_1(x) - \hat\mu_0(x)$.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    node distance=0.6cm and 1.4cm,
    box/.style={draw, rounded corners, minimum width=2.6cm, minimum height=0.8cm, font=\small, align=center},
    data/.style={box, fill=bluetint, draw=noteblue},
    model/.style={box, fill=greentint, draw=sbgreen},
    res/.style={box, fill=boxgrey, draw=extgrey, thick}
]
\node[data] (treat) {Treated rows};
\node[data, below=of treat] (ctrl) {Control rows};
\node[model, right=of treat] (m1) {$\hat{\mu}_1(x)$};
\node[model, right=of ctrl] (m0) {$\hat{\mu}_0(x)$};
\node[res, right=2.2cm of $(m1)!0.5!(m0)$] (cate) {$\hat{\tau}(x) = \hat{\mu}_1(x) - \hat{\mu}_0(x)$};
\draw[-{Stealth}] (treat) -- (m1); \draw[-{Stealth}] (ctrl) -- (m0);
\draw[-{Stealth}] (m1) -- (cate); \draw[-{Stealth}] (m0) -- (cate);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{result}{1.7}{Variances add in the T-learner}
If $\hat\mu_1$ and $\hat\mu_0$ are fitted on disjoint samples, $\Var(\hatt(x)) = \Var(\hat\mu_1(x)) + \Var(\hat\mu_0(x))$ and $\text{bias}(\hatt(x)) = \text{bias}(\hat\mu_1(x)) - \text{bias}(\hat\mu_0(x))$.
\end{result}

No penalty can shrink the effect, because $D$ is never a feature. But each arm must learn the whole baseline, usually most of the signal, from half the data.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Better Fit, Worse Effect}

Take the lapsed members, where the truth is $\mu_1 = 0.30$ and $\mu_0 = 0.10$, so $\tau = 0.20$. Two fitted T-learners:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}
\toprule
& $\widehat\mu_1$ & $\widehat\mu_0$ & \textbf{Error in each} & $\widehat\tau$ & \textbf{Error in $\tau$} \\
\midrule
T-learner A & 0.35 & 0.15 & $+0.05$, $+0.05$ & 0.20 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{0} \\
T-learner B & 0.33 & 0.07 & $+0.03$, $-0.03$ & 0.26 & \textcolor{warnred}{$+0.06$} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

B fits \emph{both} outcomes better and gets the effect \emph{worse}. The effect's error is
\[
\widehat\tau - \tau = (\widehat\mu_1 - \mu_1) - (\widehat\mu_0 - \mu_0) :
\]
the difference of two errors. Variances always add; biases cancel in A and compound in B.

\medskip

\textbf{For the effect, what matters is whether the two errors move together, not how large each one is.} Two arms fitted on different halves smooth the baseline differently, and the difference lands in $\hatt$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{X-Learner: Borrow the Other Arm}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fit $\hat\mu_0$ and $\hat\mu_1$, as in the T-learner.
  \item Impute an effect for every row, using the \emph{other} arm's model:
  \[
  \tilde\tau_i = Y_i - \hat\mu_0(X_i) \ \text{(treated rows)}, \qquad \tilde\tau_i = \hat\mu_1(X_i) - Y_i \ \text{(control rows)}.
  \]
  \item Fit $\hatt_1$ to the treated rows' imputations and $\hatt_0$ to the control rows'.
  \item Combine: $\hatt(x) = e(x)\,\hatt_0(x) + (1 - e(x))\,\hatt_1(x)$.
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip

Each imputation uses the other arm's model, so the larger arm's model does most of the work. It helps when arms are unbalanced.

\medskip

\textbf{Example.} A campaign that keeps a 10\% holdout has $e = 0.9$. Then $\hatt = 0.9\,\hatt_0 + 0.1\,\hatt_1$: most weight goes to the control rows' imputations, which use $\hat\mu_1$, fitted on the 90\% treated arm.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Learners That Target the Effect: The Transformed Outcome}

S, T, and X minimize a loss in $Y$ and get $\hatt$ as a by-product. The next three regress something whose conditional mean \emph{is} $\tau(x)$.

\begin{result}{1.8}{The transformed outcome}
With known propensity $e(x)$, let $Y^\ast = Y\,\dfrac{D - e(X)}{e(X)(1-e(X))}$. Under Assumptions 1.1 and 1.2, $\E[Y^\ast \mid X] = \tau(X)$.
\end{result}

\medskip

In the Q2 test $e = 0.5$, so $Y^\ast = 2Y$ for coupon members and $-2Y$ for the others. Every value is $-2$, $0$, or $2$, yet the segment means are the effects:
\[
\text{lapsed: } 0.5 \times 2 \times 0.30 - 0.5 \times 2 \times 0.10 = 0.20, \qquad \text{active: } 0.80 - 0.75 = 0.05 .
\]

Any regression of $Y^\ast$ on $X$ estimates the CATE. But each $Y^\ast_i$ is one outcome scaled up, so it is very noisy: among active members its standard deviation is $1.76$, against an effect of $0.05$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Doubly Robust Pseudo-Outcome}

\begin{result}{1.9}{The doubly robust pseudo-outcome}
Let
\[
\phi = \hat\mu_1(X) - \hat\mu_0(X) + \frac{D\,(Y - \hat\mu_1(X))}{\hat e(X)} - \frac{(1-D)(Y - \hat\mu_0(X))}{1 - \hat e(X)},
\]
with $\hat\mu_d$ and $\hat e$ fitted on other data. Then $\E[\phi \mid X] = \tau(X)$ if \emph{either} $\hat\mu_0 = \mu_0$ and $\hat\mu_1 = \mu_1$, \emph{or} $\hat e = e$.
\end{result}

The \textbf{DR-learner} regresses $\phi$ on $X$, with cross-fitting. The outcome models remove most of the noise; the weighted residuals correct their bias. With $\hat\mu_d = 0$, $\phi$ is $Y^\ast$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Segment} & \textbf{Effect} & \textbf{SD of $Y^\ast$} & \textbf{SD of $\phi$ (correct $\mu_d$, $e = 0.5$)} \\
\midrule
Active & 0.05 & 1.76 & 0.83 \\
Lapsed & 0.20 & 0.87 & 0.77 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The gain is largest where the baseline is high. In an experiment $e$ is known, so $\phi$ is unbiased even with poor outcome models.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The R-Learner}

\begin{result}{1.10}{The R-learner's target}
Let $\ell(x) = \E[Y \mid X=x]$. Under Assumption 1.1, $Y - \ell(X) = (D - e(X))\,\tau(X) + \varepsilon$ with $\E[\varepsilon \mid D, X] = 0$, so $\tau$ minimizes $\E\big[\{(Y - \ell(X)) - (D - e(X))\,t(X)\}^2\big]$ over functions $t$.
\end{result}

\medskip

The \textbf{R-learner} minimizes the sample version, the \textbf{R-loss}, with $\hat\ell$ and $\hat e$ fitted on other folds and a penalty on $t$. It regresses the outcome residual on the treatment residual, locally in $x$.

\medskip

At Houtan Coffee, $\ell(\text{lapsed}) = 0.10 + 0.5 \times 0.20 = 0.20$ and $\ell(\text{active}) = 0.75 + 0.5 \times 0.05 = 0.775$. Subtracting $\ell$ removes the $0.65$ baseline gap before the effect is fitted, so the prognostic variation no longer competes with the effect.

\medskip

The same idea drives causal forests and double machine learning, which come later in the course.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Shared Representation: TARNet}

Between sharing everything (S) and sharing nothing (T):

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    node distance=0.5cm and 1.1cm,
    box/.style={draw, rounded corners, minimum height=0.8cm, font=\small, align=center},
    inp/.style={box, fill=bluetint, draw=noteblue, minimum width=2.2cm},
    rep/.style={box, fill=greentint, draw=sbgreen, minimum width=2.6cm},
    head/.style={box, fill=boxgrey, draw=extgrey, minimum width=2.4cm}
]
\node[inp] (x) {14 CRM fields $x$};
\node[rep, right=of x] (phi) {shared layers\\$\Phi(x)$};
\node[head, above right=0.15cm and 1.3cm of phi] (h1) {head $h_1$: $\hat\mu_1(x)$};
\node[head, below right=0.15cm and 1.3cm of phi] (h0) {head $h_0$: $\hat\mu_0(x)$};
\draw[-{Stealth}] (x) -- (phi); \draw[-{Stealth}] (phi) -- (h1); \draw[-{Stealth}] (phi) -- (h0);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The shared layers learn the baseline, what predicts buying, once from every member.
  \item Each member trains only its own arm's head (coupon members $h_1$, the others $h_0$).
  \item $\hatt(x) = h_1(\Phi(x)) - h_0(\Phi(x))$: the heads differ only where the effect does.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{A caution.} Variants that force $\Phi(X)$ to look alike in both arms can hide an overlap failure rather than fix it. In the Q2 test overlap holds by design; in logged data it may not.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Outcome Accuracy Cannot Choose a Targeting Model}

\begin{result}{1.11}{The cost of ignoring all heterogeneity}
Under a 50/50 design, the best predictor of $Y$ that imposes a constant effect, $g(x) + \bar\tau d$, has mean squared error exceeding that of the correct model $\mu_D(X)$ by exactly $\tfrac14\Var(\tau(X))$.
\end{result}

\medskip

At Houtan Coffee, $\tfrac14\Var(\tau(X)) = \tfrac14 \times 0.0056 = 0.0014 = 0.0375^2$.

\medskip

A purchase's own variance is about $p(1-p)$, near $0.16$ here. A model can be nearly optimal by outcome loss while discarding all the heterogeneity a targeting decision needs.

\medskip

The error that matters, $\E[(\hatt(X) - \tau(X))^2]$, needs $\tau_i$, which is never observed. It can be computed only in a simulation.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Fit, Choose, Assess}

Every member gets one of three roles, assigned before any per-visit or duplicated rows are created, so that no member appears in two:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llp{7.0cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Role} & \textbf{Members} & \textbf{What happens there} \\
\midrule
Fit & training folds & Each learner estimates its nuisance models and $\hatt$ \\
Choose & randomized validation fold & Pick one learner. \textcolor{warnred}{Not by outcome RMSE} \\
Assess & held-out test & Touched once: the value of the chosen list \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

On the validation fold, compare candidates by
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the \textbf{value of the policy} each implies: the net value of its list;
  \item the R-loss; or
  \item the mean squared gap between $\hatt(X)$ and the DR pseudo-outcome $\phi$.
\end{enumerate}

When the nuisance models are accurate, (2) and (3) rank candidates as their true CATE error would.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four Targeting Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{targeting errors}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Ranking by predicted purchase.
  \item Declaring heterogeneity because one segment is significant.
  \item Choosing a CATE model by outcome RMSE.
  \item Reporting the effect at the reference group as ``the'' effect.
\end{itemize}
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one has appeared today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item marketing's purchase model loses \textyen 32{,}500 on 5{,}000 coupons;
  \item ``significant for lapsed, not for active'' says nothing about the gap; the gap's own test does;
  \item Model A's RMSE is 0.4\% worse, and its list \textyen 23{,}750 worse;
  \item the coefficient on $D$, $0.20$, is the lapsed effect, not the base's $0.125$ or the 80\%-active list's $0.08$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} Targeting needs $\tau(x)$, not $\Pr(\text{buy} \mid x)$: coupon $x$ iff $\tau(x) > \mu_1(x)/3$. A list's average effect uses that list's weights. Targeting is worth $\E[\max\{V(X), 0\}] - \max\{\E[V(X)], 0\}$.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} The Q2 draw identifies $\tau(x)$ within every $x$, but only on the test's support. A deterministic rule fails on overlap; assignment on something outside $X$ fails on independence.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} S-learners shrink the effect; T-learners learn the baseline twice and their errors subtract; the X-learner borrows the larger arm. The transformed outcome, the DR pseudo-outcome, and the R-loss target $\tau(x)$ itself; TARNet shares the baseline across arms.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} Test the gap between segments, not two significance labels, and count the segments examined.
  \item \textbf{Choose by the decision, not by the fit.} Ignoring all heterogeneity costs only $\tfrac14\Var(\tau(X))$ in outcome loss, and here \textyen 23{,}750 in value.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
