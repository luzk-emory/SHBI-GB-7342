\begin{frame}{An Estimand Chosen by the Data}

Units have covariates $X$, a randomized treatment $D$ with known $\Pr(D=1) = e$, and outcome $Y$.

\begin{defbox}{partition, leaf CATE, leaf estimate}
A \textbf{partition} $\Pi = \{\ell_1, \dots, \ell_L\}$ divides covariate space into \textbf{leaves}. The leaf CATE is $\tau_\ell = \E[Y(1) - Y(0) \mid X \in \ell]$, and its estimate on a sample $S$ is $\hatt_\ell(S) = \bar Y_{1,\ell}(S) - \bar Y_{0,\ell}(S)$.
\end{defbox}

Until today the estimand was fixed before the data: the ATE, or $\tau(x)$ for a segment we named. Now the leaves are the output of a search, so \textbf{the estimand itself depends on the data that found it}.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lp{9.4cm}}
\toprule
\spinestep{Estimand} & $\tau_\ell$ for each leaf of a partition written down before half B is opened \\
\spinestep{Identification} & half B's own coupon draw, inside each committed leaf \\
\spinestep{Estimation} & difference in means and its SE, leaf by leaf, on half B only \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The three steps still apply, but only in this order: the partition must be fixed before the data used to estimate it are consulted.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Splitting on the Outcome, Splitting on the Effect}

A regression tree (CART) splits a parent $P$ into children $L, R$ to maximize
\[
\tfrac{n_L}{n_P}(\bar Y_L - \bar Y_P)^2 + \tfrac{n_R}{n_P}(\bar Y_R - \bar Y_P)^2 .
\]
A \textbf{causal tree} replaces outcome means with effect estimates; each leaf needs both treated and control units:
\[
\Delta = \tfrac{n_L}{n_P}(\hatt_L - \hatt_P)^2 + \tfrac{n_R}{n_P}(\hatt_R - \hatt_P)^2 = \frac{n_L n_R}{n_P^2}(\hatt_L - \hatt_R)^2 ,
\]
the second form holding when $\hatt_P$ is the size-weighted average of the children.

\medskip

Region 2 at the root, pooling both arms (every split is 50/50, so each score is $\tfrac14 \times \text{gap}^2$):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Candidate split} & \textbf{Gap in $\bar Y$} & \textbf{CART score} & \textbf{Gap in $\tau$} & \textbf{Causal $\Delta$} \\
\midrule
Spending history & $0.5125 - 0.2125 = 0.30$ & 0.0225 & 0 & \textcolor{warnred}{0} \\
Customer type & $0.475 - 0.25 = 0.225$ & 0.0127 & $0.20 - 0.05 = 0.15$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{0.0056} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip

CART splits on spending; the causal tree splits on type and gives spending nothing.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why Outcome Trees Favor Prognostic Variables}

\begin{result}{1.1}{A causal criterion ignores prognostic splits}
If a split leaves the CATE unchanged ($\tau_L = \tau_R$), its population $\Delta$ is zero whatever it does to outcomes. Under randomization with $e = \tfrac12$, the outcome gap between the two sides of a split on $V$ is
\[
  \E[Y \mid V=1] - \E[Y \mid V=0] = \{\mu_0(1) - \mu_0(0)\} + \tfrac12\{\tau(1) - \tau(0)\}.
\]
\end{result}

\medskip

In Region 2, with $\mu_0$ the purchase rate without the coupon:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Split on} & \textbf{Baseline gap} & $+\ \tfrac12 \times$ \textbf{effect gap} & \textbf{Outcome gap} \\
\midrule
Spending ($V = 1$ high) & $0.45 - 0.15 = 0.30$ & $\tfrac12 \times 0 = 0$ & $0.30$ \\
Type ($V = 1$ executive) & $0.45 - 0.15 = 0.30$ & $\tfrac12(0.05 - 0.20) = -0.075$ & $0.225$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

Effect heterogeneity enters an outcome split at half weight, on top of the baseline difference. Here it even works against the modifier: executives buy more anyway and respond less, so type's outcome gap is \emph{smaller} than spending's.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Two Noisy Splits}

A discovery sample has an overall lift of $0.10$. Two candidate splits each divide it into equal halves:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Split} & \textbf{Left lift} & \textbf{Right lift} & $\Delta = 0.5(\hatt_L - 0.10)^2 + 0.5(\hatt_R - 0.10)^2$ \\

\bottomrule

\begin{center}
\Large the gap: $0.16 \;\longrightarrow\; 0.04$
\end{center}

A was chosen \emph{for} having the largest gap, and the largest gap among noisy candidates is partly noise. The more candidates, the more of the winner's gap is noise.

\begin{frame}{The Winner's Curse}

\begin{result}{1.2}{Selection inflates the winner}
Let $\hat T_1, \dots, \hat T_k$ be unbiased for $\tau_1, \dots, \tau_k$ and $j^\ast = \arg\max_j \hat T_j$. Then $\E[\hat T_{j^\ast} - \tau_{j^\ast}] \ge 0$. If the estimates are independent $N(\tau, s^2)$ with a common $\tau$, $\E[\hat T_{j^\ast}] = \tau + s\,\E[\max(Z_1, \dots, Z_k)]$ with $Z_j$ standard normal.
\end{result}

\medskip

For $k = 2, 5, 20, 100$ candidates, $\E[\max_{j \le k} Z_j] = 0.56, 1.16, 1.87, 2.51$. It grows slowly, like $\sqrt{2 \ln k}$, but it never stops growing.

\medskip

\textbf{By hand.} Take 20 candidate segments, every one with true lift $0.125$, each estimated with SE $0.04$. The best-looking one shows, on average,
\[
0.125 + 1.87 \times 0.04 = \textcolor{warnred}{0.20},
\]
above the analyst, high-spend break-even of $0.167$, in data where nothing differs. Its reported SE, $0.04$, is too small too: it ignores the search.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Honesty}

\begin{result}{1.3}{Honest leaf estimates are unbiased}
Split the data at random into $A$ and $B$, let the partition be any function of $A$ alone, and let $B$ contain its own randomized assignment. Then for every leaf with both arms present in $B$, $\E[\hatt_\ell(B) \mid \Pi] = \tau_\ell$, and the difference-in-means standard error computed on $B$ is valid given $\Pi$.
\end{result}

\bigskip

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Reported on half A}\\[0.2em]
The leaves were chosen for their large gaps. Their estimates carry the noise that selected them, and their SEs ignore the search.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Reported on half B}\\[0.2em]
Half B's noise is independent of the choice made on A. Within a leaf, B is a randomized experiment, so its difference in means is unbiased with a valid SE, as in Meetings 1 and 2.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\bigskip

The argument uses one fact: \textbf{the partition does not depend on $B$}.
\end{frame}

From the CFO's rule and Lena's log:

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Honesty Costs, and What a Split Costs}

Honesty's price is variance, because each half does only one job. If a leaf's full-sample SE is $s$, honesty raises its variance from $s^2$ to $2s^2$.

\smallskip

The alternative's price is bias. Reporting the winner from the search adds a squared bias of about $(s\,\E[\max Z_j])^2$, which exceeds $s^2$ once four or more equal candidates are compared. With 20 candidates it is $1.87^2 s^2 = 3.50\,s^2$.

\bigskip

\textbf{A split must earn its cost.} The analyst, high-spend cell, at its true rates $0.50$ and $0.30$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Members per arm} & \textbf{SE of the lift} & \\
\midrule
500 (all 4{,}000 members) & $\sqrt{0.25/500 + 0.21/500} = 0.030$ & $s$ \\
250 (one half) & $\sqrt{0.25/250 + 0.21/250} = 0.043$ & $\sqrt2\, s$ \\
125 (one half, split again) & $\sqrt{0.25/125 + 0.21/125} = 0.061$ & $2s$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip

Splitting the leaf again into two parts with the same rates gives the same effects with more noise. A split is worth making only if it can change a decision.
\end{frame}

\section{Block B: Causal forests}
% ====================================================================

\begin{frame}{From a Card to a Number per Member}

A single tree is unstable: a different random split of Region 2 could have produced different leaves. A \textbf{causal forest} grows many honest trees, each
\begin{itemize}
  \item on a random \textbf{subsample} drawn without replacement, and
  \item with a random subset of candidate variables at each split.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{2.9cm}p{4.1cm}p{5.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Method} & \textbf{Splits on} & \textbf{Reports} \\
\midrule
Outcome tree & gaps in $\bar Y$ & outcome levels; confuses prognostic with causal \\[0.2em]
Causal tree & gaps in $\hatt$ & leaf effects, inflated if estimated on the search data \\[0.2em]
Honest causal tree & gaps in $\hatt$ on half A & a readable card; unbiased leaf effects on half B \\[0.2em]
Causal forest & many honest trees & $\hatt(x)$ for every member; no card \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

A forest is best read not as an average of trees but as a way of choosing, for each target $x$, \textbf{which training units are its neighbors}.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Forests as Adaptive Neighbors}

For a target member $x$, tree $b$ places $x$ in leaf $L_b(x)$. Each tree shares a weight of $1/B$ equally among $x$'s leaf-mates; unit $i$ collects its shares:
\[
\alpha_i(x) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \frac{\ind{X_i \in L_b(x)}}{|L_b(x)|}, \qquad \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i(x) = 1 .
\]

\textbf{Four trees, an analyst with high spend as the target.} Each of $x$'s four leaves holds four training members:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccr}
\toprule
\textbf{Members} & \textbf{In how many of $x$'s leaves} & \textbf{Weight each} & \textbf{Total} \\
\midrule
1, 2 & all 4 & $\tfrac14 \times 4 \times \tfrac14 = 0.25$ & 0.50 \\
3, 4 & 2 & $\tfrac14 \times 2 \times \tfrac14 = 0.125$ & 0.25 \\
5, 6, 7, 8 & 1 & $\tfrac14 \times 1 \times \tfrac14 = 0.0625$ & 0.25 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The neighbors are \textbf{adaptive}: the trees decide which directions in $x$ matter for the effect. Splits follow effect modifiers, so $x$'s neighbors share its type; they need not share its spending.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Forest Estimate}

\begin{result}{1.4}{The forest estimate as a weighted residual regression}
For a target $x$, tree $b$ places $x$ in leaf $L_b(x)$. Define
\[
  \alpha_i(x) = \frac1B\sum_{b=1}^B \frac{\ind{X_i \in L_b(x)}}{|L_b(x)|}, \qquad \textstyle\sum_i \alpha_i(x) = 1 .
\]
The forest solves $\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\big[(Y_i - \hat\ell(X_i)) - (D_i - \hat e(X_i))\,\theta\big](D_i - \hat e(X_i)) = 0$:
\[
  \hatt(x) = \frac{\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\,(Y_i - \hat\ell(X_i))(D_i - \hat e(X_i))}{\sum_i \alpha_i(x)\,(D_i - \hat e(X_i))^2},
\]
with $\hat\ell$ and $\hat e$ estimated out of bag.
\end{result}

This is Meeting 3's R-learner, with $\ell(x) = \E[Y \mid X=x]$, localized by $\alpha_i(x)$: regress the outcome residual on the treatment residual among $x$'s neighbors.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Local Centering Removes the Baseline}

Subtracting $\hat\ell$ and $\hat e$ first is \textbf{local centering}. In Region 2 with $e = 0.5$, $\ell(x) = \mu_0(x) + 0.5\,\tau(x)$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Spending} & $\ell(x)$ & \textbf{Coupon arm: $\E[Y - \ell]$} & \textbf{No coupon: $\E[Y - \ell]$} \\
\midrule
Executive & High & $0.60 + 0.025 = 0.625$ & $+0.025$ & $-0.025$ \\
Executive & Low  & $0.30 + 0.025 = 0.325$ & $+0.025$ & $-0.025$ \\
Analyst   & High & $0.30 + 0.10 = 0.40$ & $+0.10$ & $-0.10$ \\
Analyst   & Low  & $0.00 + 0.10 = 0.10$ & $+0.10$ & $-0.10$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The residuals are $\pm\tfrac12\tau(x)$. The $0.30$ spending gap is gone; only the effect is left.

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item What prognostic variables explain is removed before any split is chosen, so splits concentrate on effect modifiers.
  \item It also protects the estimate when treatment probabilities vary with $x$, as they will in logged data later in the course.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Centering Inside a Neighborhood}

Eight analysts, equal weights $\alpha_i = \tfrac18$, $\hat e = 0.5$. By chance, the coupon arm holds more high spenders. Outcomes at their cell rates:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccr}
\toprule
& \textbf{High spend} & \textbf{Low spend} & \textbf{Mean $Y$} & \textbf{Mean $Y - \hat\ell$} \\
\midrule
Coupon (4) & 3 at 0.50 & 1 at 0.20 & $0.425$ & $+0.10$ \\
No coupon (4) & 1 at 0.30 & 3 at 0.00 & $0.075$ & $-0.10$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Without centering:} $0.425 - 0.075 = 0.35$. The spending gap leaks in: $0.20 + 0.30 \times (\tfrac34 - \tfrac14) = 0.35$.
  \item \textbf{With centering} ($\hat\ell = 0.40$ high, $0.10$ low): every residual is $\pm 0.10$, so
  \[
  \hatt(x) = \frac{\tfrac18 \times 8 \times (0.10 \times 0.5)}{\tfrac18 \times 8 \times 0.25} = \frac{0.05}{0.25} = 0.20 .
  \]
\end{itemize}

\medskip

In a randomized test such imbalance is chance: it averages out but adds variance. Where $e(x)$ varies with $x$, it is bias.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Intervals for $\tau(x)$}

\begin{block}{Wager and Athey (2018)}
Under conditions, $\hatt(x)$ is asymptotically normal, with a variance that the infinitesimal jackknife estimates:
\[
\frac{\hatt(x) - \tau(x)}{\widehat\SE(x)} \xrightarrow{d} N(0,1), \qquad \hatt(x) \pm 1.96\,\widehat\SE(x).
\]
\end{block}

The conditions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\tau$ is Lipschitz (no jumps);
  \item covariates have a density bounded away from zero on a bounded support;
  \item overlap, and honest trees whose leaves shrink in every direction;
  \item subsamples of size $s$ with $s \to \infty$ and $s/n \to 0$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Region 2's $\tau$ jumps from $0.05$ to $0.20$ between types, and its fields are binary. The conditions are a guide to when the intervals can be trusted, not a description of this data.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Three Cautions on Forest Intervals}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pointwise, not a band.} Each interval covers $\tau(x)$ at one $x$ with probability 0.95. Over thousands of members, many intervals miss.
  \item \textbf{Wide.} Intervals are usually wide relative to the spread of $\hatt(X_i)$. With an SE of $0.10$, a member's 95\% interval is $0.39$ wide, more than twice Region 2's whole gap of $0.15$ between types.
  \item \textbf{The spread of $\hatt$ overstates the spread of $\tau$}, because it includes noise. If $\hatt(X) = \tau(X) + $ independent noise with SD $0.10$:
\end{enumerate}

\[
\Var(\hatt(X)) = 0.075^2 + 0.10^2 = 0.015625 = 0.125^2 ,
\]

\begin{center}
against a true SD of $0.075$. A histogram of predicted effects is mostly noise.
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{More Trees, Same Uncertainty}

Averaging does not make trees independent: they are grown on overlapping subsamples of the same data. With per-tree variance $\sigma^2$ and pairwise correlation $\rho$, the forest's variance is
\[
\sigma^2\Big[\rho + \frac{1-\rho}{B}\Big].
\]

With per-tree SD $\sigma = 0.10$ and $\rho = 0.3$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Trees $B$} & 1 & 10 & 100 & $\infty$ \\
\midrule
\textbf{SD of $\hatt(x)$} & 0.100 & 0.061 & 0.055 & 0.055 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

More trees remove the simulation noise, $(1-\rho)/B$, and nothing else. The floor, $\sigma\sqrt\rho = 0.055$, is the uncertainty the trees share because they learn from the same members.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Variable Importance Is Not a Test}

A forest reports how often each field is used to split. Split counts describe the algorithm, not the effect:

\bigskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Correlated fields share splits arbitrarily.} Customer type comes from the job-title field. A field correlated with job title, say the share of visits on weekday mornings, can take some of type's splits, and both look moderately important.
  \item \textbf{High-cardinality fields get more chances.} A store identifier with dozens of values offers far more cut points than a binary field, so noise finds it more often.
  \item \textbf{A field can rank high because it proxies for the real modifier.} The weekday-morning field would rank high even if it modified nothing itself.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Half A already showed the risk: app use entered Lena's tree with nothing behind it. Treat a high importance as a \textbf{hypothesis for fresh data}: estimate the effect by that field on held-out members, with the SE of the gap.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Testing for Real Heterogeneity}

On held-out data, compute each member's doubly robust score $\phi_i$ (Meeting 3), which satisfies $\E[\phi_i \mid X_i] = \tau(X_i)$, and the model's prediction $\hatt(X_i)$, fitted on other data.

\begin{result}{1.5}{Best linear predictor (calibration) test}
In the population regression $\phi = \beta_1 + \beta_2(\hatt(X) - \E[\hatt(X)]) + u$,
\[
  \beta_1 = \E[\tau(X)] = \ATE, \qquad \beta_2 = \frac{\Cov(\tau(X), \hatt(X))}{\Var(\hatt(X))}.
\]
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
$\beta_2$ & \textbf{The score $\hatt$} \\
\midrule
$0$ & carries no information about effects \\
$> 0$ & detects real heterogeneity \\
$1$ & is calibrated \\
between $0$ and $1$ & is right in direction but too spread out \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Three Scores on Region 2}

Region 2's truth: $\tau = 0.20$ for half the members (analysts) and $0.05$ for the other half, so $\ATE = 0.125$ and $\Var(\tau(X)) = 0.075^2 = 0.005625$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Score} & $\beta_1$ & $\beta_2$ & \textbf{Reading} \\
\midrule
$\hatt = \tau$ & 0.125 & $1$ & calibrated \\
$\hatt = \tau + $ noise, SD $0.10$ & 0.125 & $0.005625/0.015625 = 0.36$ & right direction, too spread out \\
$\hatt$ built on app use only$^{\dagger}$ & 0.125 & $0$ & no information \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.3em}
{\footnotesize $^{\dagger}$App use is unrelated to type in Region 2.}

\medskip

$\beta_1$ is the ATE whatever the score. For the noisy score, $\Cov(\tau, \hatt) = \Var(\tau)$, so $\beta_2 = \Var(\tau)/\Var(\hatt)$: the factor by which its predictions should be shrunk toward the ATE.

\bigskip

\textbf{The test is about this score.} The app-use score has $\beta_2 = 0$, yet Region 2 has real heterogeneity by type. A non-rejection does not show heterogeneity is absent.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Sorted Group Effects (GATES)}

A forest grown on half A scores half B. Sort half B into quartiles of $\hatt$, with cut-points fixed in advance, and estimate each group's effect by difference in means:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcccrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Quartile of $\hatt$} & \textbf{Analysts} & \textbf{Coupon} & \textbf{No coupon} & \textbf{GATE} & \textbf{SE} \\
\midrule
Bottom & 10\% & 121/250 & 106/250 & 0.060 & 0.044 \\
2 & 40\% & 110/250 & 84/250 & 0.104 & 0.043 \\
3 & 60\% & 103/250 & 68/250 & 0.140 & 0.042 \\
Top & 90\% & 91/250 & 46/250 & 0.180 & 0.039 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Unbiased by Result 1.3: the grouping was fixed on other data.
  \item Effects rise from bottom to top. Top minus bottom: $0.12$, SE $\sqrt{0.044^2 + 0.039^2} = 0.059$, $z = 2.03$.
  \item The group effects average $0.121$, half B's overall lift, $425/1{,}000 - 304/1{,}000$.
  \item The top quartile is 90\% analysts: the forest has mostly rediscovered customer type.
\end{itemize}

These are the numbers a manager can act on.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Searching and Reporting}

\begin{pitfall}{searching and reporting}
Reporting a searched segment's effect from the search data; re-drawing leaves after looking at the estimation half; reading the spread of predicted effects, or variable importance, as evidence of heterogeneity.
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one appeared today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item half A reported executive app users at $0.19$; half B found $0.04$;
  \item once the leaves are re-drawn after a look at half B, half B is discovery data, and Result 1.3 no longer applies;
  \item predicted effects with noise SD $0.10$ spread with SD $0.125$, against a true $0.075$;
  \item app use entered the tree on half A with no effect behind it.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

The checks that do test heterogeneity are on held-out data: $\beta_2$ and the gap between the top and bottom GATES.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} When a search chooses the segments, the estimand is the leaf CATE $\tau_\ell$ of a partition committed before the estimation half is opened. Each line is held against its own break-even, $\tau^\ast = p_1/3$.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} Inside each committed leaf, half B's own coupon draw identifies $\tau_\ell$ (Result 1.3). It fails as soon as the partition depends on half B.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Causal trees split on effect gaps and ignore prognostic splits (Result 1.1). Forests are adaptive neighbor weights with local centering, which makes them the R-learner, localized (Result 1.4).
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} The winner of a search is inflated by about $s\,\E[\max Z_j]$ (Result 1.2). Forest intervals are pointwise and wide. Real heterogeneity is tested on held-out data, by $\beta_2$ (Result 1.5) and GATES, not by variable importance.
  \item \textbf{Find on one half, judge on the other.} Region 2's card: coupon analysts with low spend; test analysts with high spend further; no coupon for executives.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
