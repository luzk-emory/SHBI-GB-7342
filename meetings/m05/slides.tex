\begin{frame}{Value and Gain}

A \textbf{policy} is a rule $\pi: x \mapsto \{0,1\}$; the potential outcome $R(d)$ is a member's reward in money:
\[
V(\pi) = \E[R(\pi(X))], \qquad G(\pi) = V(\pi) - V(\mathbf{0}), \qquad v(x) = \E[R(1) - R(0) \mid X = x].
\]
At Houtan Coffee, $R(d) = (30 - 10d)\,Y(d)$ and $v(x) = 30\,\tau(x) - 10\,\mu_1(x)$.

\begin{result}{1.1}{The policy-value identity}
$G(\pi) = \E[\pi(X)\,v(X)] = \Pr(\pi(X) = 1)\cdot\E[v(X) \mid \pi(X) = 1]$.
\end{result}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{List} (base of 10{,}000) & \textbf{Reach $\times$ average net value} & \textbf{Gain: per member, total} \\
\midrule
Coupon nobody & $0$ & \textyen 0 \\
Coupon everyone & $1 \times \tfrac12(-6.50 + 3.00)$ & $-$\textyen 1.75, \ $-$\textyen 17{,}500 \\
Segment rule & $0.5 \times 3.00$ & $+$\textyen 1.50, \ $+$\textyen 15{,}000 \\
A random half of the lapsed & $0.25 \times 3.00$ & $+$\textyen 0.75, \ $+$\textyen 7{,}500 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Same average, half the reach, half the gain. \textbf{A model's list must beat \textyen 1.50}, the list that needed no model.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Estimating Value from a Randomized Sample}

\begin{result}{1.2}{Inverse-probability-weighted value}
Suppose $D \indep \{R(0), R(1)\} \mid X$ with known $e(x) \in (0,1)$, and let $p_\pi(X) = e(X)$ if $\pi(X) = 1$ and $1 - e(X)$ otherwise. Then
\[
  V(\pi) = \E\!\left[\frac{\ind{D = \pi(X)}}{p_\pi(X)}\,R\right], \qquad
  G(\pi) = \E\!\left[\pi(X)\left\{\frac{DR}{e(X)} - \frac{(1-D)R}{1-e(X)}\right\}\right].
\]
\end{result}

The sample version averages \textbf{row contributions} $g_i = \pi(X_i)\{D_iR_i/e(X_i) - (1-D_i)R_i/(1-e(X_i))\}$, with $\widehat\SE = \text{sd}(g_i)/\sqrt n$.

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.58\textwidth}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}lccc@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Listed buyer} & \textbf{$R$} & $e = 0.5$ & $e = 0.25$ \\
\midrule
coupon arm & 20 & $20/0.5 = +40$ & $20/0.25 = +80$ \\
control arm & $30$ & $-30/0.5 = -60$ & $-30/0.75 = -40$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.39\textwidth}
\small Non-buyers and unlisted members contribute 0. \textbf{Use the design's $e$}: at a 25\% coin a coupon-arm buyer counts $1/0.25 = 4$ times, not twice.
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\smallskip
\small Normalizing the weights within each arm (the H\'ajek version) is usually less variable.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Precise Is a List's Value?}

\begin{result}{1.3}{Variance of a row contribution}
$\Var(g_i) = \E\!\left[\pi(X)\left\{\dfrac{\E[R(1)^2 \mid X]}{e(X)} + \dfrac{\E[R(0)^2 \mid X]}{1-e(X)}\right\}\right] - G(\pi)^2$.
\end{result}

\textbf{Segment rule.} A lapsed member has $\E[R(1)^2] = 20^2(0.30) = 120$ and $\E[R(0)^2] = 30^2(0.10) = 90$, so $\Var(g_i) = 0.5(120/0.5 + 90/0.5) - 1.50^2 = 207.75$: an sd of $14.41$.

\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item The 80 rows (six $40$s, two $-60$s, 72 zeros): $\text{sd}(g_i) = 14.50$, $\widehat\SE = 14.50/\sqrt{80} = 1.62$. The estimate $1.50 \pm 3.2$ cannot separate two lists \textyen 1 apart.
  \item An evaluation fold of 5{,}000: $\SE = 14.41/\sqrt{5{,}000} = 0.20$.
  \item \textbf{Coupon everyone:} active rows add $0.5(20^2(0.80)/0.5 + 30^2(0.75)/0.5) = 995$, and the sd is $34.67$. Active members buy anyway, and each purchase enters $g_i$ at full size.
\end{itemize}

The variance comes from the reward's second moments, not from the effect. A doubly robust score (subtract a reward prediction fitted on other data) keeps the expectation and lowers the variance.
\end{frame}

\begin{result}{1.4}{Estimate rates within a group, then scale}
For a group $S$ of $n_S$ units with $n_{dS}$ in arm $d$ and arm totals $B_{dS}$, the estimator $n_S(B_{1S}/n_{1S} - B_{0S}/n_{0S})$ is unbiased, given arm sizes, for the group's incremental outcome. The raw count difference $B_{1S} - B_{0S}$ is not, unless the arms are equal, and even then it is half of it.
\end{result}

\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item Rates, then scale: $(0.40 - 0.30) \times 1{,}000 = \textcolor{sbgreen}{100}$ incremental purchases. The count difference, $216 - 138 = \textcolor{warnred}{78}$, is $540(0.40) - 460(0.30)$: with arms of 500 it would be $50$, half of 100, and the other $28$ is the larger coupon arm.
  \item Three denominators: lift per member targeted, $0.10$; per member of the fold, $Q(0.2) = 0.02$; per coupon, $30(0.10) - 10(0.40) = -$\textyen 1.00.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Profit Curves}

Replace the outcome by net value: the \textbf{profit curve} is $P(\phi) = \phi\,\E[v(X) \mid S_\phi]$.

\begin{result}{1.5}{Where the profit curve peaks}
If units are ranked by their true net value $v$ (with a continuous distribution), $P$ is concave, its slope at $\phi$ is the net value of the marginal unit, and it is maximized where that net value is zero.
\end{result}

\begin{frame}{RATE: One Number for a Ranking}

The \textbf{targeting operator characteristic}, $\text{TOC}(q) = \E[\tau(X) \mid s(X) \text{ in the top } q] - \ATE$, measures how much better the top $q$ respond than average. The \textbf{rank-weighted average treatment effect} is $\text{RATE} = \int_0^1 w(q)\,\text{TOC}(q)\,dq$. With $w(q) = 1$ it is the \textbf{AUTOC}, which emphasizes the top of the list; with $w(q) = q$ it is the \textbf{Qini coefficient}, which weighs the whole list. Averaged over the grid $q = \tfrac14, \tfrac12, \tfrac34, 1$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Ranking} & \multicolumn{4}{c}{$\text{TOC}(q)$} & \textbf{AUTOC} & \textbf{Qini} \\
& $\tfrac14$ & $\tfrac12$ & $\tfrac34$ & $1$ & & \\
\midrule
Forest (L1, A1, L2, A2) & 0.225 & 0.150 & 0.075 & 0 & 0.113 & 0.047 \\
Segment rule (lapsed first) & 0.075 & 0.075 & 0.025 & 0 & 0.044 & 0.019 \\
Random & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

A RATE significantly above zero on held-out data shows the score ranks better than random. The forest beats the segment rule on both, yet its top 5{,}000 earns \textyen 0.75 per member against \textyen 1.50: \textbf{RATE judges a ranking of effects, not the value of a list}. Use AUTOC when a small fraction will be treated, the Qini coefficient when a large one will.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Ranking Is Not Calibration}

\begin{result}{1.6}{Gain curves see only ranks}
For any strictly increasing $h$, the scores $s$ and $h \circ s$ give identical gain curves and RATEs. A threshold policy $\ind{s(x) > t}$ and its gain change with $h$.
\end{result}

\begin{frame}{Recalibrating a Score: Isotonic Regression}

A miscalibrated score can be repaired without touching its ranking. On units not used to fit the score, let $\psi_i = D_iY_i/e(X_i) - (1 - D_i)Y_i/\{1 - e(X_i)\}$, or the doubly robust score $\phi_i$ of Meeting 3; either has conditional mean $\tau(X_i)$. With $e = 0.5$, $\psi_i = 2Y_i$ in the coupon arm and $-2Y_i$ in the control arm.

\begin{result}{1.7}{Isotonic calibration}
Let $s$ be a score fixed before the calibration sample, and let $\hat h$ minimize $\sum_i\{\psi_i - h(s(X_i))\}^2$ over non-decreasing functions $h$. Then $\hat h \circ s$ never reverses the order of $s$, and $\hat h$ is constant on blocks of consecutive scores, equal on each block to the block's average of $\psi_i$.
\end{result}

\textbf{Pool-adjacent-violators} computes $\hat h$: start from each unit (or score bin) alone, and merge any neighboring pair whose averages are out of order until none is.

\medskip

Each block's value is a group effect on groups the data chose, so Meeting 4's winner's curse applies: such groups look better on the data that picked them. Fit the map on the selection fold, never on the evaluation fold.
\end{frame}

5 & 0.36 & 0.36 & 0.34 & 0.14 & coupon & coupon \\
6 & 0.48 & 0.32 & 0.34 & 0.13 & coupon & coupon \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\small
\begin{itemize}
  \item Two violations: bins 2 and 3 ($0.08 > 0.02$) pool to $0.05$; bins 5 and 6 ($0.36 > 0.32$) pool to $0.34$. The order is unchanged, so the gain curve changes only within tied bins; the scale a threshold reads does change.
  \item Bin 3 per coupon: at its raw score, $30(0.12) - 10(0.21) = +$\textyen 1.50; at its calibrated value, $30(0.05) - 10(0.21) = -$\textyen 0.60. The score overstated a lapsed group much like L2.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing and Evaluating Are Different Jobs}

\begin{result}{1.8}{The chosen policy's selection value is optimistic}
Let $\hat G_S(\pi_1), \dots, \hat G_S(\pi_K)$ be unbiased on a selection sample $S$ and $\hat\pi$ the maximizer. Then $\E[\hat G_S(\hat\pi)] \ge \E[G(\hat\pi)]$. On an evaluation sample $E$ independent of everything used to choose $\hat\pi$, $\E[\hat G_E(\hat\pi) \mid \hat\pi] = G(\hat\pi)$.
\end{result}

\textbf{An exact case.} $K$ candidate lists are each worth \textyen 1.50 per member; each selection-fold estimate is $1.50 \pm 0.50$, equally likely and independently. The winner reports \textyen 2.00 unless all $K$ drew $-0.50$, which has probability $2^{-K}$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Candidates compared, $K$} & 1 & 2 & 3 & 10 \\
\midrule
Winner's expected selection value, $1.50 + 0.50(1 - 2^{1-K})$ & 1.50 & 1.75 & 1.88 & 2.00 \\
Winner's true value & 1.50 & 1.50 & 1.50 & 1.50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\small Candidates are not only models: cut-offs, feature sets, budgets, and any adjustment after looking at results are choices too. An engine that scans ten cut-offs has chosen among ten candidates.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Freeze, Then Evaluate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    box/.style={draw, rounded corners, minimum width=3.3cm, minimum height=0.9cm, font=\small, align=center}
]
\node[box, fill=bluetint, draw=noteblue] (train) at (0,0) {Training folds\\fit $\hatt$, $\hat\mu_1$};
\node[box, fill=boxgrey, draw=extgrey] (sel) at (4.6,0) {Selection fold\\choose list, cut-off, map};
\node[box, fill=greentint, draw=sbgreen] (eval) at (9.2,0) {Evaluation fold\\value, \emph{once}};
\draw[-{Stealth}, thick] (train) -- (sel) node[midway, above, font=\scriptsize] {scores};
\draw[-{Stealth}, thick] (sel) -- (eval) node[midway, above, font=\scriptsize] {frozen $\pi$};
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Split before fitting:} draw training, selection, and evaluation samples from the experiment at random.
  \item \textbf{Fit} on training data.
  \item \textbf{Choose} among candidates on the selection sample: the list, the engine's cut-off, the isotonic map.
  \item \textbf{Freeze:} record the chosen policy's decision for every evaluation unit, and the code, before evaluation outcomes are released.
  \item \textbf{Evaluate once} and report that number.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip

A disappointing evaluation is a correct result, not a reason to return to step 3: that would turn the evaluation fold into a selection fold. The engine gets no exception.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Comparing Two Lists: Pair the Rows}

\begin{result}{1.9}{Paired comparison}
For frozen policies $\pi_a, \pi_b$ evaluated on the same randomized sample, let $d_i = g_i(\pi_a) - g_i(\pi_b)$. Then $\bar d$ is unbiased for $G(\pi_a) - G(\pi_b)$ with standard error $\text{sd}(d_i)/\sqrt n$.
\end{result}

\textbf{On the 80 rows:} segment rule minus coupon everyone. The lists agree on the 40 lapsed rows, so $d_i = 0$ there. On active rows $d_i = -g_i(\text{everyone})$: $-40$ for the 16 coupon-arm buyers, $+60$ for the 15 control-arm buyers.
\[
\bar d = \frac{-16(40) + 15(60)}{80} = 3.25 = 1.50 - (-1.75), \qquad \text{sd}(d_i) = 31.57, \qquad \widehat\SE = \frac{31.57}{\sqrt{80}} = 3.53 .
\]

Treating the two estimates as independent gives $\sqrt{1.62^2 + 3.90^2} = 4.22$ instead. Rows the lists share carry the same noise into both estimates, and it cancels in $d_i$. The more two lists overlap, the more the unpaired SE overstates the uncertainty.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Five Evaluation Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{evaluation errors}
Reporting a policy's value on the data that chose it; reusing $e = 0.5$ for a design that was not 50/50; drawing gain curves with different endpoints; comparing two lists with unpaired SEs; re-thresholding after seeing the evaluation result.
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one has a number today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item of ten lists worth \textyen 1.50 each, the winner reports \textyen 2.00 on the data that chose it;
  \item at a 25\% coin, a listed buyer contributes $+80$ or $-40$, not $+40$ or $-60$;
  \item every ranking's gain curve ends at the ATE, $0.125$; a curve that does not has been computed incorrectly;
  \item for the engine's list against the segment rule, the unpaired SE is $0.25$ and the paired SE $0.15$;
  \item a cut-off moved after the evaluation fold opens makes that fold a selection fold, and its value optimistic.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} A frozen list's gain over sending nothing, $G(\pi) = \E[\pi(X)\,v(X)]$: reach times average net value. The number to beat is the segment rule's \textyen 1.50 per member.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} A randomized fold with known $e$: weighting the rows where the coin agreed with the list recovers $V(\pi)$ and $G(\pi)$. Use the design's $e$.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Average the row contributions $\pm R/e$; their variance comes from the reward's second moments. In curves, estimate rates within a group, then scale. Gain curves and RATE judge a ranking; the profit curve prices a list and peaks where the marginal net value is zero.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} Choose on the selection fold, freeze, and evaluate once: the winner's selection value is optimistic. Compare two lists with the paired SE.
  \item \textbf{Ranking is not calibration.} A budget reads only the order; a threshold reads the scale. Isotonic regression on the selection fold repairs the scale without changing the order.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
