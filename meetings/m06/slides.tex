\begin{frame}{What Must Be True}

From the push test's log and the Q4 plan:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{6.4cm}p{6.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{The log and the plan say} & \textbf{Which buys} \\

Write $d_i \in \{0,1\}$ for the decision on member $i$. Every list today is scored by the same sum,
\[

\]
the \textbf{net value} of a push: the margin on the purchases it causes less its expected cost $c(x)$. The third sentence is what makes it a sum; Block B ends with what happens when it fails.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Unconstrained Rule}

\begin{result}{1.1}{Unconstrained rule}
Without constraints the optimal assignment is $d_i^\ast = \ind{v(X_i) > 0}$; with a uniform cost $c$ this is $\ind{\tau(X_i) > c/m}$.
\end{result}

\medskip

\textbf{A free push.} If the push and its coupon cost nothing, $v(x) > 0 \iff \tau(x) > 0$: all 10{,}000 members get one. (A push \emph{without} the coupon is a different treatment; the test never measured its effects.)

\medskip

\textbf{A hidden cost.} 1\% of pushed members switch notifications off for good, at a long-run cost of \textyen 150 each:
\[
c = 150 \times 0.01 = \text{\textyen}\,1.50, \qquad c/m = 1.50/30 = 0.05 .
\]

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & $\hatt(x)$ & $v = 30\,\hatt - 1.50$ & \textbf{Push?} \\
\midrule
Analyst & 0.20 & \textyen 4.50 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes: 5{,}000 members} \\
Executive & 0.05 & \textyen 0.00 & \textcolor{warnred}{no: exactly at the threshold} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Sure-Thing Cost}

\begin{result}{1.2}{Sure-thing decomposition}
If treatment costs $k$ per unit sent and $a$ per outcome (paid on redemption), so $c(x) = k + a\mu_1(x)$, then $v(x) = (m - a)\,\tau(x) - a\,\mu_0(x) - k$.
\end{result}

Here $k = 1.50$ and $a = 5$:

\begin{tabular}{llcccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Type} & \textbf{Spending} & $25\,\tau(x)$ & $-5\,\mu_0(x)$ & $-1.50$ & $v(x)$ \\
\midrule
Analyst & Low  & \textyen 5.00 & \textyen 0.00 & $-$\textyen 1.50 & \textyen 3.50 \\
Analyst & High & \textyen 5.00 & $-$\textyen 1.50 & $-$\textyen 1.50 & \textyen 2.00 \\

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A Cap: Rank by Net Value}

\begin{result}{1.3}{Caps: rank by net value}
Under $\sum_i d_i \le B$, an optimal assignment treats the units with the $\min(B, n_+)$ largest values of $v_i$, where $n_+$ is the number with $v_i > 0$.
\end{result}

Product caps pushes at 1{,}000 a day. On a one-day send, 5{,}000 analysts with $v > 0$ compete for 1{,}000 slots; the cells rank analyst-low first, and any 1{,}000 of them earn $1{,}000 \times 3.50 = $ \textyen 3{,}500. A causal forest (Meeting 4) splits analyst-low three ways, averaging the cell's 0.20. None buys without the push, so $v = 25\,\tau - 1.50$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrccl}
\toprule
\textbf{Forest group} & \textbf{Members} & $\hatt(x)$ & $v$ & \textbf{On the 1{,}000?} \\
\midrule
Top & 500 & 0.30 & \textyen 6.00 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{all 500} \\
Middle & 1{,}000 & 0.20 & \textyen 3.50 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{500} \\
Other & 1{,}000 & 0.15 & \textyen 2.25 & \textcolor{warnred}{none} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The forest's list earns $500 \times 6.00 + 500 \times 3.50 = $ \textyen 4{,}750. Without a cap only the sign of $v$ matters; \textbf{a binding cap makes order within the positive set worth money}, here \textyen 1{,}250.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R3: finance's memo: ¥8,000 of expected cost, at most a 5% chance of overrunning it]
\begin{frame}{A Budget: The Knapsack and Its Shadow Price}

Finance limits expected cost. With unit costs $c_i > 0$, benefits $b_i = m\tau_i$, and budget $C$: maximize $\sum_i d_i(b_i - c_i)$ subject to $\sum_i d_i c_i \le C$. The relaxation allows $d_i \in [0,1]$. Order units by $r_i = b_i/c_i$; the \textbf{greedy} assignment treats them in that order until the budget runs out or $r_i \le 1$, treating a fraction of the first unit $j$ that does not fit.

\begin{result}{1.4}{Greedy is optimal for the relaxation; the shadow price}
Let $\lambda^\ast = r_j - 1$ if the budget binds before the ratio reaches 1, and $0$ otherwise. The greedy assignment solves the relaxation and equals the rule $d_i = \ind{b_i > (1 + \lambda^\ast)c_i}$ except at the fractional unit. Where the budget binds, the optimal value $V(C)$ is concave in $C$ with slope $\lambda^\ast$: an extra yuan of budget adds $\lambda^\ast$ of net value.
\end{result}

Three consequences:
\begin{itemize}
  \item spend in order of the ratio, not of $\tau$ or $v$;
  \item a budget can be replaced by a \textbf{price}: charge each cost at $(1 + \lambda^\ast)$;
  \item $\lambda^\ast$ is the number for finance: if another use of the money returns more, the budget belongs there.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Several Coupon Depths}

With actions $a = 1, \dots, K$ of benefit $b_i(a)$ and cost $c_i(a)$, the same argument gives: each unit takes the action maximizing $b_i(a) - (1 + \lambda^\ast)c_i(a)$, or nothing if all are negative.

\medskip

Suppose the push test had also tried \textyen 10 off on analyst-low members, and 28\% bought (none buys without a push):

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Analyst-low} & $\mu_1$ & $b = 30\,\tau$ & $c = 1.50 + \text{face} \times \mu_1$ & $b - c$ & $b - 1.5\,c$ \\
\midrule
\textyen 5 off & 0.20 & \textyen 6.00 & \textyen 2.50 & \textyen 3.50 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{\textyen 2.25} \\
\textyen 10 off & 0.28 & \textyen 8.40 & \textyen 4.30 & \textcolor{sbgreen}{\textyen 4.10} & \textyen 1.95 \\
\midrule
Upgrade & & \textyen 2.40 & \textyen 1.80 & \textyen 0.60 & $-$\textyen 0.30 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No budget} ($\lambda^\ast = 0$): the deeper coupon nets more, \textyen 4.10 against \textyen 3.50.
  \item \textbf{\textyen 8{,}000 budget} ($\lambda^\ast = 0.50$): the upgrade returns $2.40/1.80 = 1.33$ per yuan, below the 1.50 of analyst-high, so analyst-low keeps \textyen 5 off. The upgrade pays only when $\lambda^\ast < 0.33$.
\end{itemize}

Tight budgets push toward cheaper actions spread over more members. A contact cap (1{,}000 pushes a day) is one more constraint, with its own shadow price.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Group Constraints}

A rule that never reads a group label $G$ can still treat groups at very different rates, because effects and costs differ by group. A \textbf{rate constraint}: $\text{rate}_a \le \gamma\,\text{rate}_b$ for groups $a, b$ of sizes $N_a, N_b$, with $\gamma \ge 1$. (The four-fifths guideline of US employment practice is $\gamma = 1.25$.)

\begin{result}{1.5}{Group constraints give group-specific thresholds}
\small
Under a cap $\sum_i d_i \le B$ and $\sum_{i \in a} d_i/N_a \le \gamma\sum_{i \in b} d_i/N_b$, the relaxation is solved by ranking units by $v_i$ within each group and treating $i \in a$ if $v_i > \lambda^\ast + \eta^\ast/N_a$ and $i \in b$ if $v_i > \lambda^\ast - \gamma\eta^\ast/N_b$ (other groups at $\lambda^\ast$), where $\lambda^\ast, \eta^\ast \ge 0$ are the multipliers of the cap and the rate constraint. Where both bind, the optimal value $V(\gamma)$ rises with $\gamma$ at rate $\eta^\ast\,\text{rate}_b$.
\end{result}

The group the unconstrained rule favors faces a higher bar, the other a lower one, and $\eta^\ast$ sets the gap. The \textbf{price of fairness} $V(\infty) - V(\gamma)$ is the value given up: the value of the members swapped.

\smallskip

Which constraint to impose (parity of rates, of expected benefit, of outcomes) is a value judgment, not an estimation problem, and writing a protected label into the rule may itself be restricted. The multiplier tells management what a constraint costs.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Price of Fairness in Region 2}

Region 2 has two districts, North with 6{,}000 members and South with 4{,}000. The send is one day of 1{,}000 pushes. The rule never reads the district, but the members it values most live mostly in the North:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{District} & \textbf{Members at each net value} & \textbf{Everyone else} \\
\midrule
North ($a$), 6{,}000 & 840 at \textyen 5.00 & below \textyen 2.50 \\
South ($b$), 4{,}000 & 160 at \textyen 5.00; 1{,}000 at \textyen 2.50 & below \textyen 2.50 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}\small
  \item \textbf{Unconstrained:} the 1{,}000 at \textyen 5.00. Rates $840/6{,}000 = 14\%$ and $160/4{,}000 = 4\%$; value \textyen 5{,}000.
  \item \textbf{With $\gamma = 1.25$:} $\tfrac{n_a}{6{,}000} = 1.25\,\tfrac{n_b}{4{,}000}$ and $n_a + n_b = 1{,}000$ give $n_b = 347.8$, $n_a = 652.2$; in whole members, 348 South and 652 North (rates 10.9\% and 8.7\%).
  \item The list swaps $840 - 652 = 188$ North members at \textyen 5.00 for 188 South members at \textyen 2.50: value $5{,}000 - 188 \times 2.50 = $ \textyen 4{,}530, a price of \textyen 470.
\end{itemize}

The thresholds are \textyen 5.00 in the North and \textyen 2.50 in the South. Solving $\lambda^\ast + \eta^\ast/6{,}000 = 5.00$ and $\lambda^\ast - 1.25\,\eta^\ast/4{,}000 = 2.50$ gives $\eta^\ast \approx 5{,}217$ and $\lambda^\ast \approx 4.13$. Tightening $\gamma$ from 1.25 to 1.20 costs about $0.05 \times 5{,}217 \times 0.087 \approx$ \textyen 23.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Spending Risk}

The budget constrains \emph{expected} cost. With redemption $R_i \sim \text{Bernoulli}(s_i)$, $s_i = \mu_1(X_i)$, member $i$ costs $k + aR_i$: \textyen 6.50 or \textyen 1.50.

\begin{result}{1.6}{Mean, variance, and the reserve}
If redemptions are independent, realized cost $K = \sum_i d_i(k + aR_i)$ has $\E[K] = \sum_i d_ic_i$ and $\Var(K) = a^2\sum_i d_is_i(1-s_i)$. Under the normal approximation, $\Pr(K > C) \le \alpha$ if and only if $\E[K] + z_\alpha\sqrt{\Var(K)} \le C$.
\end{result}

\textbf{Finance:} if every analyst is pushed, what is the chance of spending over \textyen 18{,}000? $\E[K] = 2{,}500(4.00) + 2{,}500(2.50) = $ \textyen 16{,}250,
\[
\text{sd}(K) = \sqrt{25\,[2{,}500(0.25) + 2{,}500(0.16)]} = \text{\textyen}\,160, \qquad z = (18{,}000 - 16{,}250)/160 = 10.9 :
\]
essentially zero. The 95th percentile of cost is $16{,}250 + 1.645 \times 160 = $ \textyen 16{,}513.

\smallskip

\small The \textbf{reserve} $z_\alpha\,\text{sd}(K)$ grows like the square root of the list, expected cost like the list. It covers Bernoulli luck, not a wrong cost model or a common shock (a cold week) that moves every redemption together.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Uncertain Effects: The Conservative Rule}

If $\hat v(x) \approx N(v(x), \SE(x)^2)$, the \textbf{conservative rule} treats when $\hat v(x) - z_\alpha\SE(x) > 0$.

\begin{result}{1.7}{What the conservative rule guarantees}
For any $x$ with $v(x) \le 0$, the conservative rule treats it with probability at most $\alpha$.
\end{result}

\textbf{Bound the net value, not $\tau$}: the cost is estimated too. Here $v = 25\,\mu_1 - 30\,\mu_0 - 1.50$, and with arms estimated independently $\widehat\Var(\hat v) = 25^2\,\widehat\Var(\hat\mu_1) + 30^2\,\widehat\Var(\hat\mu_0)$ (Meeting 2).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Cell} & $\hat v$ & \textbf{SE of each arm} & $\widehat\SE(\hat v)$ & \textbf{Bound} ($z = 1.645$) \\
\midrule

\begin{frame}{When the Mean Is Not Enough: Capacity}

Net value $m\tau(x) - c(x)$ is linear in the outcome. When the payoff is not, the average effect no longer decides. Make the unit the holder of the capacity: a store on a push day.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Its baristas can serve $q = 400$ orders a day.
  \item Each order served earns $p = $ \textyen 30; each turned away costs $g = $ \textyen 20 (an apology voucher).
  \item A day is worth $\pi(y) = p\min(y, q) - g(y - q)^+$; pushing the store's nearby members costs $c = $ \textyen 500.
\end{itemize}

\begin{result}{1.8}{A nonlinear payoff needs each arm's distribution}
The promotion's net value at $x$ is $v(x) = \E[\pi(Y(1)) \mid X = x] - \E[\pi(Y(0)) \mid X = x] - c$. In a randomized experiment each term is identified from one arm, $\E[\pi(Y(d)) \mid X = x] = \E[\pi(Y) \mid D = d, X = x]$. So $v(x)$ depends on the conditional distributions of $Y(1)$ and $Y(0)$ separately; it does not depend on their joint distribution, and it is not a function of $\tau(x)$ alone unless $\pi$ is linear ($q = \infty$ gives $v = p\tau(x) - c$).
\end{result}
\end{frame}

\textbf{The linear rule} says $p\tau - c = 30 \times 60 - 500 = $ \textyen 1{,}300 for both: push both.

\medskip

\textbf{The payoff}, with $\pi(360) = 10{,}800$, $\pi(420) = 12{,}000 - 400 = 11{,}600$, and $\pi(480) = 12{,}000 - 1{,}600 = 10{,}400$:
\begin{itemize}
  \item store P: $v = 11{,}600 - 10{,}800 - 500 = $ \textcolor{sbgreen}{\textyen 300}, so push;
  \item store Q: $v = \tfrac12(10{,}400 - 10{,}800) + \tfrac12(0) - 500 = $ \textcolor{warnred}{$-$\textyen 700}, so do not push.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Same CATE, opposite decisions: Q's busy days run past capacity and cost vouchers. What the decision does \emph{not} need is the distribution of individual effects $Y(1) - Y(0)$, which no experiment identifies.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Choosing the Capacity: The Critical Fractile}

Store Q sets its capacity $q$ (staff, stock) for a push day before demand is known, losing $c_u$ per order it cannot fill and $c_o$ per unit left idle. $F_1(\cdot \mid x)$ is the distribution function of $Y(1)$ given $X = x$.

\begin{result}{1.9}{The critical fractile}
The expected loss $c_u\E[(Y(1) - q)^+ \mid x] + c_o\E[(q - Y(1))^+ \mid x]$ is minimized at the $\kappa$-quantile of $F_1(\cdot \mid x)$, $\kappa = c_u/(c_u + c_o)$.
\end{result}

$c_u = 30 + 20 = $ \textyen 50 (margin plus voucher), $c_o = $ \textyen 10 (stock and labor wasted), so $\kappa = 50/60 = 0.83$. As $F_1(360) = 0.5 < 0.83 \le F_1(480) = 1$, plan for 480. Expected loss:
\begin{itemize}
  \item at 420 (baseline plus the CATE), $\tfrac12 \times 50 \times 60 + \tfrac12 \times 10 \times 60 = $ \textcolor{warnred}{\textyen 1{,}800} a day;
  \item at 480 (the 0.83-quantile), $\tfrac12 \times 10 \times 120 = $ \textcolor{sbgreen}{\textyen 600} a day.
\end{itemize}

\small $\kappa$ comes from the business: it prices the randomness of the outcome, while $z_\alpha$ guards against error in the estimate. The $\kappa$-quantile of $Y(1)$ given $x$ is a one-arm prediction (quantile regression or a quantile forest).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Net value $v(x) = m\tau(x) - c(x)$; without constraints, push iff $v(x) > 0$.
  \item A coupon paid on redemption charges sure things, so the same lift can be worth \textyen 3.50 or \textyen 2.00.
  \item A cap ranks by $v$; a budget ranks by $b/c$, and $\lambda^\ast = 0.50$ prices it. Tight budgets favor shallow coupons.
  \item A rate constraint sets group-specific thresholds; in Region 2 it costs \textyen 470.
  \item The reserve covers Bernoulli luck (\textyen 184); a lower bound on $v$ covers noisy estimates.
  \item With a capacity, each arm's distribution matters, so plan at the critical fractile, not at baseline plus the CATE.
\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}{Every Rule So Far Plugs In}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[
    block/.style={draw, rounded corners, minimum width=4.2cm, minimum height=1.2cm, font=\small, align=center}

\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Block A was \textbf{predict-then-optimize}: estimate $\tau$ (and $c$), then hand the estimates to an optimizer.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The estimates are trained for accuracy, with a loss that penalizes errors on every member.
  \item The decision is judged by value, and only errors that change a decision cost anything.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

The two criteria need not agree: two equally accurate models can lose \textyen 0 or \textyen 5{,}000.
\end{frame}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Both have the same mean squared error over the cells, $\tfrac14 \times 0.10^2 = 0.0025$.

\end{itemize}

\medskip

An estimation loss buys accuracy everywhere, including where the decision is never in doubt. A decision loss counts only the errors that flip a decision, weighted by what is at stake.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Three Routes on One Spectrum}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Predict, then optimize} (plug-in). Fit $\hatt$ by an estimation loss and apply the optimizer. Modular and reusable, but accuracy is bought everywhere.
  \item \textbf{Decision-focused learning.} Keep the optimizer; train $\hatt_\theta$ on the value of the decision it induces, $\frac1n\sum_i \pi_\theta(X_i)\Gamma_i$ ($\Gamma_i$ is the score of the next frame). The objective jumps as decisions flip, so practice uses smooth surrogates (SPO+) or randomized optimizers. Worth it when the optimizer couples units: a budget, a capacity, a menu of coupon depths.
  \item \textbf{Policy learning.} Drop the estimate and search a class $\Pi$ of rules, such as depth-two trees, for the one with the highest estimated value. A restricted class is easy to explain, audit, and deploy.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip

Route 2 is route 3 with the class $\Pi = \{\text{optimizer} \circ \hatt_\theta\}$.

\medskip

\textbf{The causal twist.} Textbook predict-then-optimize trains on observed labels (realized demand). Here the label, $\tau_i$, is never observed. A doubly robust score takes its place: a noisy draw whose conditional mean is the net value.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Doubly Robust Scores for Net Value}

\begin{result}{1.10}{Empirical welfare maximization with doubly robust scores}
Let $\Gamma_i = m\,\phi_i - c(X_i)$, where $\phi_i$ is the cross-fitted doubly robust score of Meeting~3. Then $\E[\Gamma_i \mid X_i] = v(X_i)$, so $\frac1n\sum_i \pi(X_i)\Gamma_i$ is unbiased for the gain $G(\pi)$ of any fixed policy, and
\[
\hat\pi = \arg\max_{\pi \in \Pi} \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n \pi(X_i)\,\Gamma_i .
\]
\end{result}

With $e = 0.5$, $\phi_i = \hat\mu_1 - \hat\mu_0 + 2D_i(Y_i - \hat\mu_1) - 2(1 - D_i)(Y_i - \hat\mu_0)$. Analyst-high: $\hat\mu_1 = 0.50$, $\hat\mu_0 = 0.30$, $c = 4.00$.

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Policy Learning Is Weighted Classification}

\begin{result}{1.11}{Policy learning is weighted classification}
For any policy $\pi$, with $\Gamma_i^- = \max(-\Gamma_i, 0)$,
\[
  \sum_{i=1}^n \pi(X_i)\Gamma_i = \sum_{i=1}^n |\Gamma_i|\,\ind{\pi(X_i) = \ind{\Gamma_i > 0}} - \sum_{i=1}^n \Gamma_i^- .
\]
Maximizing estimated value is minimizing the misclassification of the sign of $\Gamma_i$, each unit weighted by $|\Gamma_i|$.
\end{result}

Any classifier that accepts sample weights becomes a policy learner (\textbf{outcome-weighted learning}). An estimation loss penalizes errors on every unit; this loss penalizes only errors that flip a decision, weighted by how much is at stake.

\medskip

Under a budget, replace $\Gamma_i$ by $\Gamma_i - \lambda^\ast c(X_i)$ (Result 1.4).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Deploying So That It Can Still Be Checked}

A deployed policy is a hypothesis about where value lies.

\medskip

\textbf{Keep a random holdout.} Hold back 10\% of each listed cell, at random, with no push: 250 analyst-low and 39 analyst-high, 289 members.
\begin{itemize}
  \item It forgoes $250 \times 3.50 + 39 \times 2.00 = $ \textyen 953 of expected net, about a tenth of the list's \textyen 9{,}532.
  \item It keeps measuring the list's value, detects drift, and generates experimental data for the Q1 decision.
  \item A list deployed without one destroys the data needed to evaluate it.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{Log the assignment probability.} A listed member is pushed with probability 0.9, so the logs are an experiment with known propensities, and the inverse-probability weighting of Meeting 5 applies: a pushed buyer counts $1/0.9 = 1.11$ times, a held-out buyer $1/0.1 = 10$ times. Members off the list have probability 0, so nothing in the Q4 logs speaks to them.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bandits: Experimentation That Never Stops}

A fixed test spends half its traffic on the worse arm until the end. A \textbf{multi-armed bandit} shifts traffic toward arms that look better as data arrive. \textbf{Thompson sampling} assigns each new unit to an arm with probability equal to the current posterior probability that the arm is best.

\medskip

Two texts for the seasonal-drink push, after 200 pushes each:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Text} & \textbf{Purchases} & \textbf{Posterior (uniform prior)} & \textbf{Share of the next pushes} \\
\midrule
1 & 50 of 200 & Beta(51, 151) & 0.88 \\
2 & 40 of 200 & Beta(41, 161) & 0.12 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

$\Pr(\text{text 1 is better} \mid \text{data}) = 0.88$, so the next push carries text 1 with probability 0.88. A fixed test would still send half to text 2. Log every probability: the logs are then experimental data with known, varying propensities.

\medskip

\small Bandits suit many short-lived decisions (push texts); a one-off strategic decision, such as whether to run the Q4 push, is better served by a clean test.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What the Sum Assumes}

Every list today was scored by $\sum_i d_i v(X_i)$. That assumes \textbf{no interference} and one well-defined treatment. At the coffee chain it can fail:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Telling:} a pushed member brings a friend to try the seasonal drink.
  \item \textbf{Drawing demand:} a push near one store takes orders from the next one.
  \item \textbf{Sharing a capacity:} members pushed near the same store share its 400 orders a day, so each member's value depends on who else is pushed.
\end{itemize}
Then the objective is not separable and none of the sorting results holds.

\bigskip

\textbf{Check support before ranking.} If the push test had held no executive-low controls, a model would still print a $\hat\mu_0$ for them, and the optimizer would treat that number as data. It cannot tell a measured number from an extrapolated one (Meeting 3).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Allocation Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{allocation errors}
Ranking by $\tau$ when costs differ by unit; spending the budget in order of effect rather than ratio; planning capacity at baseline plus the CATE; treating a fairness constraint as free; reporting the optimizer's own estimate of the list's value; deploying a rule with no holdout; ignoring spillovers when a list is scaled up.
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each has a number today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item analyst-low and analyst-high share a lift of 0.20 but are worth \textyen 3.50 and \textyen 2.00;
  \item the budget funds analyst-low first, by ratio 2.40 against 1.50;
  \item store Q planned at 420 expects to lose \textyen 1{,}800 a day, at 480 only \textyen 600;
  \item the rate constraint in Region 2 costs \textyen 470;
  \item the learned tree claims \textyen 6.88 a member and earns \textyen 0.88;
  \item a 10\% holdout costs \textyen 953 and keeps the list checkable.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} The net value of a list, $\sum_i d_i v(X_i)$ with $v(x) = m\tau(x) - c(x)$, under the business's constraints; for a learned rule, its gain $G(\pi)$. With a capacity, the expected payoff in each arm.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} The push test's coin identifies $\tau(x)$ cell by cell; transport to Q4 rests on the same push, coupon, and members; the sum rests on no interference. A nonlinear payoff needs each arm's distribution, never the joint.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} The benefit is a contrast, the cost a one-arm prediction. A cap ranks by $v$, a budget by $b/c$ with shadow price $\lambda^\ast$, a rate constraint by group-specific thresholds, a capacity at the critical fractile. Policy learning maximizes $\frac1n\sum_i \pi(X_i)\Gamma_i$: a weighted classification.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} A reserve covers spending luck; a lower bound on $v$ covers noisy effects; a learned rule's own estimate is optimistic and its regret shrinks with $n$; a holdout keeps the deployed rule checkable.
  \item \textbf{Judge a model by the decisions it changes.} Equal accuracy can cost \textyen 0 or \textyen 5{,}000.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
