\section{Block A: Identification and adjustment}
% ====================================================================

% [RELEASE R1: the Q3 program, sales' table and Lena's four sentences]
\begin{frame}{The Program on Trial}

In Q3 the chain's automated campaign engine from Meeting 5 sent 5{,}000 \textyen 10 coupons, to half of the 10{,}000-member base. It chose members by a score built from recency, tier, and spend, throttled by a daily contact cap.

\bigskip

The CFO wants the program reviewed. Sales has pulled the Q3 logs:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Got coupon} & \textbf{No coupon} \\
\midrule
Members & 5{,}000 & 5{,}000 \\
Purchase rate & 0.40 & 0.62 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\bigskip

Sales' reading: \emph{``The coupon repels customers. Kill it.''}

\medskip

The CFO will not fund another experiment. \textbf{The Q3 logs must answer.}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Number That Decides It}

A purchase earns $m = $ \textyen 30 of margin. The coupon costs $c = $ \textyen 10, paid on every purchase a holder makes, not only on the purchases it caused. Per coupon sent to last quarter's recipients,
\[
\text{net} = \underbrace{30\,\tau}_{\text{purchases the coupon caused}} - \underbrace{10 \times 0.40}_{\text{coupons redeemed}} .
\]

The recipients' purchase rate, $0.40$, is observed: for a recipient, $Y = Y(1)$.

\begin{defbox}{break-even effect}
\[
\tau^\ast = \frac{c\,\E[Y(1) \mid D=1]}{m} = \frac{10 \times 0.40}{30} = 0.133 .
\]
\end{defbox}

\medskip

Sales' number is $-0.22$. Every estimate today is a lift, and you want to know \textbf{which side of $0.133$} it lands on.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Logs Produced by a Targeting Rule}

Treatment $D$ (coupon), outcome $Y$ (purchase), pre-treatment covariates $X$ (the CRM fields):
\[
\mu_d(x) = \E[Y \mid D=d, X=x], \qquad e(x) = \Pr(D=1 \mid X=x).
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item When a recorded rule assigned treatment, $e$ is that rule, viewed as a function of its inputs.
  \item That includes a rule written by an automated system. Its decisions are the assignment mechanism, and the logs it leaves are observational however sophisticated it was. The engine did not remove confounding; it wrote it down.
  \item The ATE, ATT, and ATU differ whenever effects vary with $X$ and the rule's targeting depends on $X$. Which one is needed is a question about the decision.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

The CFO's question, ``keep the program as it ran?'', is about the members who got coupons: the \textbf{ATT}, held against $\tau^\ast = 0.133$.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R2: the two-segment table; pooled and within-segment differences are computed before the next frame]

\medskip

For the rest of Block A, treat segment as the rule's only input. Block B opens the full export.
\end{frame}

Recipients are $0.2$ active and $0.8$ lapsed. Split the pooled difference with the selection-bias decomposition from Meeting 1:
\small
\begin{align*}

\medskip

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Must Be True}

\begin{assumption}{1.1}{Conditional exchangeability}
$\{Y(0), Y(1)\} \indep D \mid X$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{1.2}{Overlap}
$0 < e(x) < 1$ for every $x$ in the population the estimand refers to.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{1.3}{Consistency}
$Y = Y(D)$: one version of treatment, no interference.
\end{assumption}

\medskip

For the ATT a weaker pair suffices: $Y(0) \indep D \mid X$ and $e(x) < 1$. Recipients' $Y(1)$ is observed; only their $Y(0)$ must be borrowed from non-recipients with the same $x$.

\medskip

Randomization (Meetings 1 to 6) delivered the first two by design. Today they must be \textbf{argued}.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Assumptions, Sentence by Sentence}

From Lena's description of the Q3 program:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{6.4cm}p{6.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Lena's description says} & \textbf{Which buys} \\
\midrule
``Coupons were assigned by the scoring rule and by nothing else.'' & Exchangeability: nobody hand-picked recipients on something unrecorded. \\[0.4em]
``Every field the rule read is in the export.'' & Exchangeability: $X$ contains the rule's inputs. \\[0.4em]
``The daily cap meant no score band was sent coupons on every day.'' & Overlap: within a band, some members got a coupon and some did not. \\[0.4em]
``One coupon design, one channel, one redemption rule.'' & Consistency. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\textbf{Exchangeability cannot be tested} from the data it concerns. With a rule it reads: the rule's inputs are all in $X$, and whatever the rule left to chance was unrelated to outcomes. Defend it by finding out how decisions were actually made, not by inspecting the data.

\medskip

Overlap is checkable, and it is where a decisive rule fails. Block B checks the third sentence.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Standardization}

\begin{result}{1.4}{Standardization (the g-formula)}
Under Assumptions 1.1--1.3, $\E[Y(d)] = \E_X[\mu_d(X)]$, so $\ATE = \E_X[\mu_1(X) - \mu_0(X)]$ and $\ATT = \E[Y - \mu_0(X) \mid D = 1]$.
\end{result}

\medskip

Within each $x$ the arms are comparable, so the within-cell difference is a causal effect; average the cells with the weights of the population the estimand refers to.

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The ATT form needs only $\mu_0$, evaluated at the recipients' $x$: exactly where non-recipients may be scarce.
  \item With two segments, $\mu_0$ and $\mu_1$ are four cell means and standardization is arithmetic.
  \item With 14 fields, $\mu_d$ must be modeled (\textbf{g-computation}). The model fills in every $x$, including those where one arm is empty, and will not report where it is extrapolating.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Which Average Does the CFO Need?}

The same two cell effects, $0.05$ and $0.20$, weighted three ways:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{llrl}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimand} & \textbf{Weights (active / lapsed)} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Answers} \\
\midrule
ATE & everyone: $0.5 / 0.5$ & $0.125$ & ``coupon everyone?'' \\
ATT & recipients: $0.2 / 0.8$ & $\mathbf{0.17}$ & ``keep the program as run?'' \\
ATU & non-recipients: $0.8 / 0.2$ & $0.08$ & ``extend it to the rest?'' \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The CFO's question is the second:
\[
\ATT = 0.17 > \tau^\ast = 0.133: \qquad \text{net} = 30 \times 0.17 - 4 = +\text{\textyen}\,1.10 \text{ per coupon}.
\]

Couponing everyone is a different estimand with a different break-even, because everyone would redeem more: $\E[Y(1)] = 0.55$, so $\tau^\ast = 10 \times 0.55/30 = 0.183 > 0.125$. It loses \textyen 1.75 per member, as in Meeting 3.

\medskip

\begin{center}
\textbf{Say which average you estimated, and for whom.}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Weighting Instead of Modeling}

\begin{result}{1.5}{The IPW identity}
Under Assumptions 1.1--1.3, $\E[DY/e(X)] = \E[Y(1)]$ and $\E[(1-D)Y/(1-e(X))] = \E[Y(0)]$. Under the weaker ATT conditions,
\[
  \ATT = \frac{\E[DY] - \E\big[\tfrac{e(X)}{1-e(X)}(1-D)Y\big]}{\Pr(D=1)} .
\]
\end{result}

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Active, coupon} & \textbf{Lapsed, coupon} & \textbf{Active, none} & \textbf{Lapsed, none} \\
\midrule
Members & 1{,}000 & 4{,}000 & 4{,}000 & 1{,}000 \\
Weight $1/e$ or $1/(1-e)$ & $1/0.2 = 5$ & $1/0.8 = 1.25$ & $1/0.8 = 1.25$ & $1/0.2 = 5$ \\
Weighted members & 5{,}000 & 5{,}000 & 5{,}000 & 5{,}000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

Each arm now looks like the whole base, half active and half lapsed: $\widehat\E[Y(1)] = \tfrac12(0.80 + 0.30) = 0.55$, $\widehat\E[Y(0)] = \tfrac12(0.75 + 0.10) = 0.425$, and the difference is $0.125 = \ATE$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Weighting for the ATT, and How Many Members It Rests On}

For the ATT, recipients keep weight one; non-recipients get the odds $e/(1-e)$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Non-recipients} & \textbf{Members} & $e$ & \textbf{Weight $e/(1-e)$} & \textbf{Weighted members} \\
\midrule
Active & 4{,}000 & 0.2 & 0.25 & 1{,}000 \\
Lapsed & 1{,}000 & 0.8 & 4 & 4{,}000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\[
\widehat\E[Y(0) \mid D=1] = \frac{1{,}000(0.75) + 4{,}000(0.10)}{5{,}000} = 0.23, \qquad \widehat\ATT = 0.40 - 0.23 = 0.17 .
\]

\textbf{H\'ajek form:} divide by the sum of the weights within each arm, as here. It is invariant to shifting $Y$ and usually less variable.

\textbf{Effective sample size} of an arm's weights, $(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$:
\[
\frac{5{,}000^2}{4{,}000(0.25^2) + 1{,}000(4^2)} = \frac{25{,}000{,}000}{16{,}250} = 1{,}538 \text{ of } 5{,}000 .
\]
The ATT's comparison rests mostly on the 1{,}000 lapsed non-recipients. (With ATE weights, each arm's is 3{,}200 of 5{,}000.)
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why One Number Can Replace All of $X$}

\begin{result}{1.6}{The balancing property (Rosenbaum and Rubin)}
$D \indep X \mid e(X)$; hence if exchangeability holds given $X$, it holds given $e(X)$ alone.
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Two members with the same $e(x)$ were treated alike by the rule, whichever of the 14 fields produced that value. Comparing them is as good as comparing members with identical $x$.
  \item Adjusting for the scalar $e(X)$, by weighting, stratifying, or matching recipients to non-recipients with similar $e$, does the work of the full vector.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{The propensity model is not a prediction task.} Its job is to reproduce the assignment mechanism and balance $X$. A model that predicts the coupon almost perfectly is reporting that the rule was nearly deterministic: a failure of overlap, not a success.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Regression Adjustment Estimates}

$Y = \alpha + \tau D + X^\top\beta + \varepsilon$ is standardization with two restrictions: $\mu_0$ linear in $X$ (else residual confounding is attributed to $D$), and a constant effect (else the coefficient matches none of the three estimands).

\begin{result}{1.7}{Variance-weighted estimand}
If $\mu_0(X) = X^\top\beta$ exactly and effects vary, the OLS coefficient on $D$ converges to $\E[\tau(X)\,e(X)(1-e(X))]/\E[e(X)(1-e(X))]$.
\end{result}

On the Q3 logs, regress purchase on the coupon and a lapsed dummy. $\mu_0$ is exactly linear in the dummy, so Result 1.7 applies. Each segment's weight is $n_s e_s(1 - e_s) = 5{,}000 \times 0.2 \times 0.8 = 800$, the same for both, so
\[
\hatt_{\text{OLS}} = \tfrac12(0.05 + 0.20) = 0.125 .
\]

The control is right but the estimand is wrong. Since $0.125 < 0.133$, the regression says \textcolor{warnred}{kill}; the ATT, $0.17$, says \textcolor{sbgreen}{keep}. The weights are largest where the rule was least decided ($e$ near $0.5$). Interacting $D$ with centered $X$, or standardizing, targets the ATE or ATT instead.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Good and Bad Controls}

Exchangeability is a statement about a particular $X$, and enlarging $X$ can destroy it (the backdoor criterion from Meeting 1).

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lllp{5.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Column in the export} & \textbf{Type} & \textbf{Include?} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
Recency, tier, past spend & confounder & \textcolor{sbgreen}{yes} & the rule's inputs, which also predict buying \\
App open in the coupon week & mediator & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & coupon $\to$ open $\to$ purchase: removes part of the effect \\
Redeemed the coupon & post-treatment & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & a version of the outcome \\
In the Q3 survey sample & collider & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & sampled on coupon \emph{and} recent purchase \\
Send day under the cap & predicts $D$ only & \textcolor{warnred}{no} & within band and day the coupon is all or nothing: no overlap left \\
Store of first visit & predicts $Y$ only & optional & pre-treatment; can tighten the estimate \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The test is not ``is it correlated with $Y$?'': the mediator and the collider both are. Measure controls before the treatment decision, and justify each by its causes, not only its date. ``Everything in the warehouse'' almost always contains post-treatment variables.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The App-Open Column}

A junior analyst: \emph{``Members who never open the app cannot see the coupon. Restrict to app openers; it makes the comparison fairer.''}

\bigskip

Take a clean case: 1{,}000 members, coupon randomized 50/50, and a coupon with no effect at all.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Half the members have high intent and buy regardless; half have low intent and never buy.
  \item A member opens the app if they received a coupon or have high intent.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Members} & \textbf{Open the app} & \textbf{Buy} \\
\midrule
Coupon, high intent & 250 & 250 & 250 \\
Coupon, low intent & 250 & 250 & 0 \\
No coupon, high intent & 250 & 250 & 250 \\
No coupon, low intent & 250 & 0 & 0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\textbf{What is the coupon ``effect'' among app openers?}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Conditioning on a Collider}

Among app openers:
\[
\Pr(\text{buy} \mid \text{coupon, open}) = \frac{250}{500} = 0.50, \qquad
\Pr(\text{buy} \mid \text{no coupon, open}) = \frac{250}{250} = 1.00 .
\]

\begin{center}
\Large ``Effect'' $= \textcolor{warnred}{-0.50}$ \quad for a true effect of $\mathbf{0}$.
\end{center}

The coupon was randomized: there was no confounding to fix. Opening the app is caused by the coupon \emph{and} by intent, so restricting to openers keeps every recipient but only the high-intent non-recipients (the collider result from Meeting 1).

\medskip

\textbf{Timing is necessary but not sufficient.} A variable recorded \emph{before} the coupon is still a collider if two unrecorded causes, one of $D$ and one of $Y$, both drive it (\textbf{M-bias}). Justify each control by its causes, not only its date.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item The engine's rule is the assignment mechanism, and $e(x)$ is the rule. It confounds by construction: $-0.22$ pooled, $+0.05$ and $+0.20$ within segment.
  \item Exchangeability is a claim about how decisions were made; overlap is checkable.
  \item Standardization and IPW identify the same quantities from the outcome side and the assignment side. The CFO needs the ATT: $0.17 > 0.133$, \textyen 1.10 per coupon.
  \item The ATT rests on 1{,}538 effective non-recipients of 5{,}000. One number, $e(x)$, can stand in for all of $X$.
  \item Regression with the right control returns $0.125$: a variance-weighted average, and the wrong decision.
  \item Choose controls by timing and cause: the app-open column manufactures $-0.50$ from nothing.
\end{itemize}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Reconstruct the Rule}

\begin{frame}{Doubly Robust Estimation}

\begin{result}{1.8}{AIPW and double robustness}
Let
\[
  \phi = \tilde\mu_1(X) - \tilde\mu_0(X) + \frac{D\,(Y - \tilde\mu_1(X))}{\tilde e(X)} - \frac{(1-D)(Y - \tilde\mu_0(X))}{1 - \tilde e(X)}
\]
for working models $\tilde\mu_d$ and $\tilde e$. Under Assumptions 1.1--1.3, $\E[\phi] = \ATE$ if \emph{either} $\tilde\mu_d = \mu_d$ for both $d$ \emph{or} $\tilde e = e$.
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The first two terms are standardization; the weighted residuals correct what the outcome model got wrong.
  \item Given $X$, the error is a sum of \textbf{products}: the outcome model's error times the propensity model's relative error. Each product vanishes if either model is right.
  \item With flexible models, the estimator's error depends on the product of the two models' errors; with cross-fitting, which comes later in the course, it has a valid normal approximation where neither IPW nor standardization alone does.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What Double Robustness Does Not Cover}

\textbf{Modeling errors, not missing variables.} Double robustness protects against a wrong $\tilde\mu_d$ or a wrong $\tilde e$. If exchangeability fails, say store managers added unrecorded ``VIP'' members to the list, every estimator today is biased: standardization, IPW, regression, and AIPW alike.

\bigskip

\textbf{Inference.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Bootstrap over members, re-estimating $\hat e$ (and $\hat\mu_d$) in every replicate: the propensity is estimated, and its noise belongs in the interval.
  \item If members share a store whose shocks move them together, resample stores as blocks.
  \item Or use the cross-fitted AIPW standard error, which comes later in the course.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Trimming and the design record apply to AIPW too: its weighted residuals carry the same $1/\tilde e$ and $1/(1 - \tilde e)$, and band 4's 50 members carry the same weight.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{How Well Does Adjustment Work?}

LaLonde (1986) took a job-training experiment, replaced its randomized control group with survey respondents, and asked whether adjustment recovered the experimental answer.

\begin{itemize}
  \item The regressions of the time missed badly, sometimes with the wrong sign.
  \item Later work (Dehejia and Wahba 1999, and others) found that propensity methods do well when the comparison group overlaps the treated \emph{and} the covariates include prior values of the outcome, and badly otherwise.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

\textbf{The covariates matter more than the estimator.}

\medskip

At Houtan Coffee, the Q3 review stands on two facts: the export holds every field the score read, past purchases among them, and within a band the cap, not the members, decided who got a coupon. The choice among IPW, standardization, and AIPW matters less.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A First Look at Sensitivity}

If an unmeasured $U$ shifts the purchase rate by $\gamma$ and differs between recipients and non-recipients with the same $X$ by $\delta$, a linear adjustment is off by about $\gamma\delta$. Ask the reverse question: \textbf{how large must $\gamma\delta$ be to move the estimate across the break-even?}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Conclusion} & \textbf{Estimate} & \textbf{Break-even} & \textbf{$\gamma\delta$ that overturns it} \\
\midrule
Keep the program as run & 0.17 & 0.133 & 0.037 \\

Stop coupons to active members & 0.05 & 0.267 & 0.217 \\

\medskip

A hidden confounder a tenth as strong as segment overturns ``keep as run''; stopping the active coupons survives one over half as strong. None needs a confounder stronger than segment, so the conclusions rest on Lena's first sentence. A formal omitted-variable sensitivity analysis, later in the course, makes this precise.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Adjustment Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{adjustment errors}
Adjusting for post-treatment or collider variables; trimming without restating the estimand; choosing the specification after seeing the outcome; treating a near-perfect propensity model as good news; believing double robustness covers unmeasured confounders.
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each one has appeared today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the app-open column turns a coupon with no effect into $-0.50$;
  \item trimmed, the coupon loses \textyen 0.73 per recipient; for the program as run it earns \textyen 1.10;
  \item the design record was written with the purchase column locked;
  \item band 4's $\hat e = 0.98$ is the model working, and the overlap failing;
  \item with both working models wrong, AIPW returns the pooled $-0.22$, and no model fixes an unrecorded VIP list.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} Keeping the program as it ran needs the ATT, held against its own break-even ($0.133$). Couponing everyone is the ATE against $0.183$. A trimmed analysis estimates the ATT of a different population, with a different break-even.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} The engine's rule is the assignment mechanism and $e(x)$ is the rule. Exchangeability is a claim about how decisions were made; overlap is checked in the coupon column; controls are chosen by timing and cause, not correlation.
  \item \spinestep{Design.} Reconstruct the rule, check overlap, fix the trimming rule and the estimand, check balance, write it down; only then open the outcomes.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Standardization and IPW are one identification seen from two sides; regression returns a variance-weighted average; AIPW is right if either model is. Thin overlap makes standardization extrapolate silently and weights explode visibly.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} Report the effective sample size; bootstrap with $\hat e$ re-estimated; ask how strong a hidden confounder would have to be.
  \item \textbf{Every estimate describes a population.} Say which: here the answer is ``keep for lapsed members, stop for active ones''.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
