% [RELEASE R1: a simulator of Qiantan Hotels]
\begin{frame}{Meeting 8 Left One Doubt}

Wendy, commercial director of Qiantan Hotels (80 properties), was asked to raise midweek premium rates by 8\%, from \textyen 800 a night. An occupied room costs \textyen 200, so its contribution rises from \textyen 600 to \textyen 664.

\[
\text{break-even elasticity} \;=\; \frac{\ln(600/664)}{\ln 1.08} \;=\; -1.32 .
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item Meeting 8's double machine learning (DML) on 87{,}600 hotel-nights gave an elasticity of $-1.56$, past the break-even. \textbf{Do not raise.}
  \item It assumes the logged rates were as good as random given the 47 pricing signals.
  \item Managers override the revenue-management (RM) system on about 8\% of nights, on local knowledge (a wedding block, a conference). The override is logged; the reason is not.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Two generative-AI proposals reach Wendy. A \textbf{simulator} learned from Qiantan Hotels' logs, to test estimators and pricing engines (Block A). A \textbf{language model} that reads guest reviews and turns text into variables (Block B). For each, the question is the course's: \emph{where does the truth come from, and how is it checked?}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{From Standardization to a Simulator}

The g-formula (Meeting 7) computes $\E[Y(d)]$ by averaging $\mu_d(x)$ over $X$. A \textbf{learned simulator} extends this from averages to distributions. It fits a generative model in pieces that follow the causal order:

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small},
    box/.style={draw=noteblue, fill=bluetint, rounded corners=1pt, minimum height=0.85cm, align=center, text width=3.0cm}]
  \node[box] (x) at (0,0) {$\hat p(x)$\\47 signals of a night};
  \node[box] (d) at (4.4,0) {$\hat p(d \mid x)$\\the rate posted};
  \node[box] (y) at (8.8,0) {$\hat p(y \mid d, x)$\\premium bookings};
  \draw[->] (x) -- (d); \draw[->] (d) -- (y);
  \draw[->] (x.north) to[bend left=18] (y.north);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Each piece can be any generative model: a parametric fit, a mixture, a normalizing flow, a variational autoencoder (VAE), a boosted tree with a fitted noise distribution.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Sample $x$, then $d$, then $y$: \textbf{synthetic logs}.
  \item Sample $y$ for a \emph{chosen} $d$: potential outcomes. The simulator can then evaluate any policy, including a nightly optimizer or a sequential rule that has no simple estimator.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Qiantan Hotels' data-science team fitted one to the 87{,}600 hotel-nights: given a night's 47 signals and a rate, it draws a number of premium bookings.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What the Simulator's Answer Rests On}

\begin{result}{1.1}{A simulator's effects are g-formula effects}
If $\hat p(x) = p(x)$ and $\hat p(y \mid d, x) = p(y \mid d, x)$, the simulator's average outcome under action $d$ is $\sum_x \E[Y \mid D=d, X=x]\,p(x)$. This equals $\E[Y(d)]$ if and only if the g-formula does, that is, under consistency, conditional exchangeability given $X$, and overlap.
\end{result}

\medskip

\textbf{At Qiantan Hotels.} ``Bookings at a different rate'' is causal only if, in the real logs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the rate was as good as random given the 47 signals, measured as of pricing time (Meeting 8's assumption);
  \item every rate asked about was posted, on similar nights, somewhere in the logs (overlap).
\end{itemize}

\medskip

The overrides are exactly where the first fails. A simulator fitted to logs reproduces the observed joint distribution and still gets effects wrong if an unmeasured confounder drove the rate: the selection bias of Meeting 1 is built into $\hat p(y \mid d, x)$.
\end{frame}

\begin{tabular}{lrr}

\textbf{Category} & \textbf{Price} & \textbf{Demand} \\
\midrule

Standard & 40 & 100 \\

Premium & 70 & 210 \\

\bottomrule

\end{center}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Simulator 1 never sees category. It fits demand to price: the pooled slope, $+2.0$ per unit of price.
  \item Simulator 2 conditions on category. Within each category the slope is $-1.0$.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\medskip

Simulator 1's synthetic nights look like the real ones: same means, same spread, the same positive association. Ask each what happens if every price rises by 10. Simulator 1 says demand rises by 20; Simulator 2 says it falls by 10.

\medskip

Had category not been recorded, only Simulator 1 could be built, and every comparison of synthetic with real logs would pass. The overrides' local events are such a variable. \textbf{A simulator's realism is evidence about the data, not about causation.}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Planting the Truth}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Planting an effect.} Keep $\hat p(x)$ and a control-outcome model; define the other outcomes from a chosen effect, often calibrated to reproduce a past randomized test. The team planted an elasticity of $-1.50$ on every night, so an 8\% rise multiplies a night's bookings by $1.08^{-1.50} = 0.891$.
  \item \textbf{Planting confounding.} Add a latent $U$ that raises both the chance of treatment and the outcome, with chosen strengths, and hide it from the analyst. The team planted the overrides: on 8\% of nights an unrecorded local event raises demand by 12\%, and the manager raises the rate by 10\%.
\end{itemize}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{6.2cm}p{6.2cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Taken from the logs} & \textbf{Chosen by the analyst} \\
\midrule
the 47 signals' joint distribution; the shape of demand across nights; how the RM rule set rates; the noise & the elasticity $-1.50$; the override share (8\%), its demand effect (+12\%), its rate effect (+10\%) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

So the simulator cannot tell Wendy her guests' true elasticity: the one inside it was planted. It can test whether a method recovers a known truth on data shaped like hers.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{An Estimator Testbed}

Generate many datasets, run an estimator on each, compare with the planted truth. The team drew 200 datasets with the real logs' hotels, nights, and signals. The truth is $-1.50$ because they planted it.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimator} & \textbf{Overrides planted?} & \textbf{Mean estimate} & \textbf{Coverage} \\
\midrule
Log-log OLS, no controls & no & $+0.42$ & 0.00 \\
OLS, hotel and six occupancy bands & no & $-1.18$ & 0.02 \\
DML, boosted-tree nuisances & no & $-1.49$ & 0.94 \\
DML, lasso nuisances & no & $-1.41$ & 0.71 \\
DML, boosted-tree nuisances & \textbf{yes} & $-1.37$ & 0.38 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

Coverage is the share of the 200 nominal 95\% intervals that contain $-1.50$. The testbed checks code and statistics on \textbf{this business's data shape}: skewed demand, rare event nights, a step-function pricing rule, nights pinned to the rule. A textbook model, $y = a + bx + \varepsilon$, has none of them.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Reading the Testbed}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lcc}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimator} & \textbf{Bias}: mean $+ 1.50$ & \textbf{Coverage} \\
\midrule
Log-log OLS, no controls & $+1.92$ & 0.00 \\
OLS, hotel and six occupancy bands & $+0.32$ & 0.02 \\
DML, boosted trees & $+0.01$ & 0.94 \\
DML, lasso & $+0.09$ & 0.71 \\
DML, boosted trees, overrides planted & $+0.13$ & 0.38 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Boosted trees beat lasso because the RM rule is a step function of bucketed signals with interactions. Lasso fits it with smooth linear terms, part of the rule stays in the residuals, and coverage falls to 0.71.
  \item With the overrides planted, DML is pulled toward zero by 0.13. If the real overrides act like the planted ones, the truth is near $-1.56 - 0.13 = -1.69$, further past $-1.32$. The decision does not change.
  \item The testbed cannot tell you whether the real overrides look like the planted ones. Their size was \emph{chosen}. Turning that dial and reporting the decision at each setting is a sensitivity analysis, not a measurement.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Pipeline Testbed}

Businesses deploy \textbf{pipelines} (a model feeding an optimizer feeding a decision), not estimators.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Generate synthetic logs, with planted effects and, if wanted, planted confounding.
  \item Run each candidate pipeline exactly as in production: fit, optimize, output a policy.
  \item Evaluate each policy on the simulator's potential outcomes, where its value is known.
  \item Compute the \textbf{oracle} policy from the planted truth, and each pipeline's \textbf{regret}, the oracle value minus the pipeline value.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small}, box/.style={draw=noteblue, fill=bluetint, rounded corners=1pt, minimum height=0.8cm, align=center, text width=2.6cm}]
  \node[box] (real) at (0,0) {Real logs and past tests};
  \node[box] (sim) at (3.75,0) {Simulator\\(planted truth)};
  \node[box] (syn) at (7.5,0) {Synthetic logs};
  \node[box] (pipe) at (11.25,0) {Pipeline:\\model $\to$ optimizer};
  \node[box] (pol) at (11.25,-1.55) {Policy};
  \node[box, draw=sbgreen, fill=greentint, text width=5.0cm] (reg) at (5.2,-1.55) {Value on true potential outcomes; regret vs oracle};
  \draw[->] (real) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize]{fit} (sim);
  \draw[->] (sim) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize]{sample} (syn);
  \draw[->] (syn) -- (pipe); \draw[->] (pipe) -- (pol);
  \draw[->] (pol) -- (reg); \draw[->, dashed] (sim.south) -- node[left, font=\scriptsize]{truth} (sim.south |- reg.north);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\small The pipeline never sees the truth; only the evaluation does. At Qiantan Hotels, the policy is a rate for every hotel-night, its value is contribution, $(\text{rate} - 200) \times \text{bookings}$, and the oracle posts the rate that maximizes the planted contribution.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Regret Counts Only Misclassified Units}

Take a two-action decision: raise a night's rate by 8\% or not. $v(x)$ is the net value of raising on a night with signals $x$; $\pi(x) \in \{0, 1\}$ is a policy.

\begin{result}{1.2}{Regret counts only misclassified units}
Without a budget, the oracle treats $x$ if and only if its net value $v(x) > 0$. For any policy $\pi$,
\[
  V(\pi^\ast) - V(\pi) = \E\big[\,|v(X)|\;\ind{\pi(X) \ne \pi^\ast(X)}\big].
\]
\end{result}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Mistakes near the break-even cost little; mistakes on units with a large $|v(x)|$ cost a lot.
  \item A pipeline can estimate effects poorly and still have low regret if its errors fall where $|v|$ is small.
  \item This is why decision-focused learning and policy learning (Meeting 6) train on the decision's value rather than on estimation error.
\end{itemize}

With a continuous rate the logic is the same: regret is the contribution lost where the pipeline's rate departs from the oracle's.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Check 1: Backtests Against Past Interventions}

The simulator should reproduce, out of sample, the measured effects of past experiments or policy changes. This is the \textbf{only check on its causal part}.

\bigskip

Six months ago a randomized test offered loyalty members a rate 8\% lower on nights a coin chose. It measured $+4.8$ extra member bookings per chosen night from the rate (SE 1.3). The team re-ran the test inside each simulator, with managers' overrides as logged:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Reproduced} & \textbf{Gap from $+4.8$} & \textbf{Gap in SEs} \\
\midrule
Boosted simulator & $+4.6$ & 0.2 & 0.2 \\
Neural simulator & $+3.4$ & 1.4 & 1.1 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Neither simulator is rejected: both gaps are within about one SE. The backtest is weak evidence and does not separate them.
  \item It covers rates near the current ones. It says nothing above \textyen 1{,}920, where the pipelines differ most. A backtest earns trust only in the region it covers.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Checks 2 and 3: Support and Compounding Error}

\textbf{Staying within support.} A simulator will produce bookings for rates never seen in the logs, without warning.
\begin{itemize}
  \item 14\% of the vendor's rates lie above \textyen 1{,}920, the 95th percentile of the logged rates, where the logs are thin and both simulators largely extrapolate the shapes they learned. On those nights the regret compares one model with another, not with reality.
  \item Keep evaluated policies within the training range, or cap rates at it; treat answers outside it as extrapolation.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{Compounding error.} In sequential simulators, small one-step errors accumulate.
\begin{itemize}
  \item A simulator that rolls bookings forward day by day over the booking window feeds each day's error into the next day's pace. Validate whole trajectories, not one step.
  \item An optimizer compounds error too. Choosing every night the rate with the highest \emph{predicted} contribution selects the rates where the prediction is most optimistic: the winner's curse of Meeting 4. The realized loss exceeds the demand model's average error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Check 4: Home Advantage}

A pipeline whose models share the simulator's model class tends to look good because it matches the data-generating process.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small},
    box/.style={draw, rounded corners=1pt, minimum height=0.7cm, align=center, text width=3.3cm}]

  \node[box, draw=sbgreen, fill=greentint, text width=3.4cm] (bs) at (5.6,0.6) {Boosted simulator:\\vendor's regret \textyen 380};
  \node[box, draw=warnred, fill=redtint, text width=3.4cm] (ns) at (5.6,-0.6) {Neural simulator:\\vendor's regret \textyen 2{,}240};
  \node[box, draw=extgrey, fill=boxgrey, text width=3.4cm] (vd) at (11.2,0.6) {Vendor's model:\\boosted trees};
  \draw[->] (logs.east) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize, sloped]{boosted trees} (bs.west);
  \draw[->] (logs.east) -- node[below, font=\scriptsize, sloped]{neural net} (ns.west);
  \draw[<->, dashed, sbgreen] (bs) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize]{same errors} (vd);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Same class, same logs: the vendor's errors are the boosted simulator's errors, and the evaluation cannot see them.
  \item The neural simulator does not share them: there the vendor's regret is the worst of the three.
  \item Build simulators of more than one class; trust only rankings that hold across them. DML pricing beats the current rule on both.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\medskip

The vendor's report: \emph{``Demand is inelastic. Raise rates.''} At $-0.9$ the rise would pay; at $-1.56$ it would not.

\bigskip

In the simulator, the team planted the truth on top of Qiantan Hotels' own logs. Here nobody planted it: the elasticity is \textbf{whatever the model asserts}.
\end{frame}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{2.6cm}p{5.0cm}p{5.0cm}}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Documented problems: too little variance, stereotyped subgroups, sensitivity to wording, agreement with published results that reflects memorization.
  \item Legitimate uses: drafting survey questions or a test's design, choosing price points, setting a prior for a power calculation. It settles nothing causal unless validated out of sample against real experiments in a similar setting.
  \item The \textbf{causal effect VAE (CEVAE)}, which infers a hidden confounder from proxies, is on the asserted side too: its identification rests on structure it does not state.
\end{itemize}

The $-0.9$ is a hypothesis. What settles it is a design: a randomized rate test.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{End of Block A}

\begin{itemize}
  \item A learned simulator is a g-formula with distributions: its effects rest on consistency, conditional exchangeability, and overlap in the logs it learned from.
  \item Realism is evidence about the data, not about causation. The analyst plants the effect ($-1.50$) and the confounding (the overrides).
  \item Estimator testbed: DML with boosted trees is unbiased here; planted overrides pull it 0.13 toward zero.
  \item Pipeline testbed: regret against the oracle counts only misclassified units, weighted by $|v|$. DML pricing has the smallest worst case, \textyen 760.
  \item Trust a simulator through backtests, within support, across model classes.
  \item A silicon sample's truth is asserted, not planted: $-0.9$ is not evidence.
\end{itemize}

% [RELEASE R5: the Q3 rate test and the review labels]
\begin{frame}{The Q3 Rate Test}

Wendy did not take the rise on trust. In Q3, at 40 hotels, \textbf{a coin chose each hotel-week's midweek premium rate}: the current one or 8\% higher.

\begin{itemize}
  \item Bookings, the main outcome: on higher-rate weeks, midweek premium bookings were 12.4\% lower (SE 1.2\%).
  \item \textbf{Guardrail}: stop the rise if the share of reviews complaining about price or value goes up by more than 5 points.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

Nobody can read 10{,}000 reviews. A language model labels each one as complaining about price or value, or not. Trained staff also labeled a random 200 reviews per arm: the \textbf{gold labels}.

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Higher rate} & \textbf{Current rate} \\
\midrule
Reviews & 5{,}000 & 5{,}000 \\
Share the model labels ``complaint'' & 0.22 & 0.15 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip

On the model's labels the rise added $0.22 - 0.15 = 0.07$, or 7 points, which trips the guardrail.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Language Models as Measurement Instruments}

A language model turns text into variables: a review's sentiment, whether a review complains about value, an embedding of a listing page. Each is a \textbf{measurement with error}, and the error's consequence depends on the variable's role.

\bigskip

For a binary true variable $Y$ and model label $f$:

\begin{defbox}{error rates of a model label}
False-positive rate $\alpha = \Pr(f = 1 \mid Y = 0)$; \quad false-negative rate $\beta = \Pr(f = 0 \mid Y = 1)$.
\end{defbox}

\medskip

At Qiantan Hotels, $Y = 1$ if a trained reader says a review complains about price or value, and $f = 1$ if the model says so. The model's share $\E[f]$ is not the complaint rate $\E[Y]$, and the gap between arms of $\E[f]$ need not be the effect on $\E[Y]$.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Model-Coded Outcome}

\begin{result}{1.3}{A model-coded outcome}
If $(\alpha, \beta)$ are the same in both arms (non-differential error) and $\alpha + \beta < 1$, then
$\E[f \mid D=1] - \E[f \mid D=0] = (1 - \alpha - \beta)\{\E[Y \mid D=1] - \E[Y \mid D=0]\}$. If they differ by arm, $(\alpha_d, \beta_d)$,
\[
  \E[f \mid D=1] - \E[f \mid D=0] = (1-\alpha_1-\beta_1)p_1 - (1-\alpha_0-\beta_0)p_0 + (\alpha_1 - \alpha_0),
\]
with $p_d = \E[Y \mid D=d]$.
\end{result}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Non-differential} error shrinks the effect toward zero by the factor $1 - \alpha - \beta$.
  \item \textbf{Differential} error arises whenever the treatment changes the text in ways that affect the model's accuracy. It biases the effect in either direction, and the term $\alpha_1 - \alpha_0$ can create an effect from nothing.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

A higher rate plausibly changes what guests write. Whether it changed the model's accuracy is a question for the gold labels.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Gold Subsample}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{200 gold reviews per arm} & \textbf{Higher rate} & \textbf{Current rate} \\
\midrule
Gold complaints, model says yes & 34 & 26 \\
Gold complaints, model says no & 2 & 2 \\
Gold non-complaints, model says yes & 12 & 4 \\
Gold non-complaints, model says no & 152 & 168 \\
\midrule
True complaint rate $p_d$ & $36/200 = 0.18$ & $28/200 = 0.14$ \\
Complaints the model catches, $1 - \beta_d$ & $34/36 = 0.944$ & $26/28 = 0.929$ \\
Non-complaints the model flags, $\alpha_d$ & $12/164 = 0.073$ & $4/172 = 0.023$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The true effect on complaints is $0.18 - 0.14 = 0.04$, not $0.07$.
  \item The model catches complaints equally well in both arms, but flags \textbf{three times as many} non-complaints on higher-rate weeks.
  \item Why: more reviews mention the price without complaining (\emph{``worth it even at this price''}), and the model flags them. The treatment changed the text, and with it the model's error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Differential Error, Decomposed}

On the gold reviews the model says $46/200 = 0.23$ and $30/200 = 0.15$: a gap of $0.08$, twice the true $0.04$. Result 1.3 splits it exactly:
\[
\underbrace{(1 - 0.073 - 0.056)(0.18) - (1 - 0.023 - 0.071)(0.14)}_{\text{scaled true rates: } 0.030}
\;+\; \underbrace{(0.073 - 0.023)}_{\alpha_1 - \alpha_0:\; 0.050} \;=\; 0.08 .
\]

\bigskip

Had the error been non-differential, with the current arm's rates in both arms, the model-labeled effect would be
\[
(1 - \alpha_0 - \beta_0) \times 0.04 = \Bigl(\tfrac{26}{28} - \tfrac{4}{172}\Bigr) \times 0.04 = 0.905 \times 0.04 = 0.036 :
\]
attenuated toward zero. The model's actual $0.07$ is \textbf{inflated}: differential error manufactured about 0.03 of it.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lll}
\toprule
\textbf{Error} & \textbf{Effect on the estimate} & \textbf{Here} \\
\midrule
Non-differential & shrinks by $1 - \alpha - \beta$ & $0.036$ \\
Differential & either direction, any size & $0.07$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Model-Coded Effect Modifier}

\begin{result}{1.4}{A model-coded effect modifier}
If a binary modifier $V$ splits 50/50 and the model's coded version $\tilde V$ has symmetric misclassification rate $q < \tfrac12$, then
$\tau(\tilde V = 1) - \tau(\tilde V = 0) = (1 - 2q)\{\tau(V = 1) - \tau(V = 0)\}$.
\end{result}

\medskip

\textbf{Illustration.} Suppose the Q3 test's 40 hotels are 20 business and 20 leisure hotels, with elasticities $-1.0$ and $-2.0$. A model codes each hotel's type from its listing page, wrongly for 10\% of hotels: $q = 0.10$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Elasticity by} & \textbf{Business} & \textbf{Leisure} & \textbf{Gap} \\
\midrule
true type & $-1.0$ & $-2.0$ & $1.0$ \\
coded type & $0.9(-1.0) + 0.1(-2.0) = -1.1$ & $0.1(-1.0) + 0.9(-2.0) = -1.9$ & $0.8$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The coded gap is $(1 - 2 \times 0.10) \times 1.0 = 0.8$. Each coded group mixes in the other group's effect, so the heterogeneity shrinks toward zero. Code a modifier only from text fixed before treatment: the listing page as of the test, not the reviews it produced.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Labels by Role}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{p{2.3cm}p{4.6cm}p{5.6cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{Coded variable} & \textbf{Consequence of error} & \textbf{Direction} \\
\midrule
Outcome & non-differential: shrinks by $1 - \alpha - \beta$ & toward zero \\
& differential: adds $\alpha_1 - \alpha_0$ and more & either way; can create an effect from nothing \\[0.3em]

In every role, accuracy must be \textbf{measured} against gold labels, separately by arm or group, and the coded field must be built from text fixed before treatment.

\medskip

A validation on pre-treatment text says nothing about the reviews the treatment produced: at Qiantan Hotels, the false-positive rate tripled only in the higher-rate arm.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Correcting With a Gold Subsample}

Let a model label all $N$ units ($f_i$) and humans label a random subsample of $n$ ($Y_i$).

\begin{result}{1.5}{Prediction-powered inference (PPI) for a mean}
\[
  \hat\theta_{\text{PP}} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N f_i + \frac1n\sum_{i \in \text{gold}}(Y_i - f_i)
\]
is unbiased for $\E[Y]$ whatever the model's quality. If the gold sample is drawn independently of the $N$ units used for the first term, $\Var(\hat\theta_{\text{PP}}) = \Var(f)/N + \Var(Y - f)/n$.
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item The second term, the \textbf{rectifier}, removes the model's average error.
  \item A better model makes $Y - f$ less variable and the interval narrower; a worse one widens it but never biases it.
  \item For an effect on a coded outcome, apply PPI \textbf{in each arm}, with gold labels drawn in each arm: that is what catches differential error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{PPI on the Q3 Reviews}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
& \textbf{Higher rate} & \textbf{Current rate} \\
\midrule
Model's rate on all 5{,}000 & 0.22 & 0.15 \\
Rectifier: gold minus model, on the 200 & $0.18 - 0.23 = -0.05$ & $0.14 - 0.15 = -0.01$ \\
\textbf{PPI estimate} & \textbf{0.17} & \textbf{0.14} \\
$\widehat\Var(f)$; \ $\widehat\Var(Y - f)$ & $0.22 \times 0.78$; \ 0.0675 & $0.15 \times 0.85$; \ 0.0299 \\
$\widehat\SE = \sqrt{\widehat\Var(f)/5{,}000 + \widehat\Var(Y - f)/200}$ & 0.019 & 0.013 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Effect on complaints: $0.17 - 0.14 = 0.03$, SE $\sqrt{0.019^2 + 0.013^2} = 0.023$, 95\% interval $[-0.015, 0.075]$.
  \item Gold labels alone: $0.18 - 0.14 = 0.04$, SE $\sqrt{0.18 \times 0.82/200 + 0.14 \times 0.86/200} = 0.037$.
  \item The gold reviews are a subsample of the 5{,}000, not independent of them, so the SE is a slight approximation.
\end{itemize}

The guardrail is \textbf{neither tripped nor cleared}: 3 points is below 5, but the interval reaches 7.5.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Why PPI Works Here}

\textbf{Unbiased, although the error differs by arm.} Each arm's rectifier is estimated from a random subsample \emph{of that arm}, so it carries that arm's own $\alpha_d$ and $\beta_d$. The corrected rate is unbiased whatever the error looks like, provided the gold labels are right. Gold labels from the current arm alone would have applied its correction, $-0.01$, to both arms: the effect would stay at $0.07$.

\bigskip

\textbf{More precise than gold alone.} The 5{,}000 model labels carry the level; the gold labels estimate only a small correction.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Variance per review} & \textbf{Higher rate} & \textbf{Current rate} \\
\midrule
Outcome, $p(1-p)$ & $0.18 \times 0.82 = 0.15$ & $0.14 \times 0.86 = 0.12$ \\
Rectifier, $\Var(Y - f)$ & 0.0675 & 0.0299 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\textbf{Design-based supervised learning (DSL)} extends the idea to regression coefficients and to gold samples drawn with known unequal probabilities: for example, over-sampling reviews the model is unsure about.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R6: text as controls]
\begin{frame}{Embeddings as Controls}

Text often carries confounders that structured fields miss. A hotel's listing page reveals its positioning (renovated rooms, a rooftop bar, a business center), which drives both its rates and its bookings. An \textbf{embedding} of the text can enter double machine learning as part of $X$.

\bigskip

Back to Meeting 8's DML on the logs, with text added to the controls:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Controls} & \textbf{Log-rate variance left} & \textbf{Elasticity (SE)} \\
\midrule
47 signals & 9\% & $-1.56$ (0.03) \\
+ embeddings of listing pages, as of each night & 6\% & $-1.58$ (0.04) \\
+ embeddings of that night's guest reviews & 1\% & $-0.97$ (0.09) \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

The listing pages barely move the estimate. The reviews move it by 0.59, toward zero, and past the break-even of $-1.32$. Two failure modes explain the last row.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Failure 1: Leakage}

Text written or edited \textbf{after treatment} contains the treatment or its consequences: a bad control.

\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[>={Stealth[length=2mm]}, every node/.style={font=\small},
    v/.style={draw, rounded corners=1pt, minimum height=0.7cm, align=center, text width=2.2cm}]
  \node[v, draw=noteblue, fill=bluetint, text width=2.5cm] (L) at (0,0) {Listing page\\(as of the night)};
  \node[v, draw=sbgreen, fill=greentint] (D) at (3.7,0) {Rate};
  \node[v, draw=sbgreen, fill=greentint] (Y) at (7.3,0) {Bookings};
  \node[v, draw=warnred, fill=redtint] (R) at (11.2,0) {That night's reviews};
  \draw[->] (L) -- (D); \draw[->] (D) -- (Y);
  \draw[->] (L.north) to[bend left=15] (Y.north);
  \draw[->, warnred] (D.south) to[bend right=15] node[below, font=\scriptsize]{value perceptions} (R.south);
  \draw[->, warnred] (Y) -- node[above, font=\scriptsize]{crowding} (R);
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Listing pages as of each night are written before the rate. They proxy for quality, a confounder, so controlling for them is legitimate.
  \item That night's reviews are written after the stay, caused by the rate and by bookings, so they encode the outcome. Controlling for them absorbs part of the effect, as a mediator would, and opens collider paths. The estimate moves to $-0.97$.
\end{itemize}

Reviews posted after a price change are post-treatment, however predictive they are.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Failure 2: Overlap Collapse}

A rich embedding can predict treatment almost perfectly, because text describes what drove treatment. The treatment residual vanishes and the estimate rests on a few units.

\bigskip

DML uses only the variation in the rate left after the controls, so its SE scales roughly as $1/\sqrt{\text{share left}}$:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Controls} & \textbf{Share left} & \textbf{Factor on the SE} & \textbf{SE} \\
\midrule
47 signals & 9\% & 1 & 0.03 \\
+ listing pages & 6\% & $\sqrt{9/6} = 1.22$ & $0.03 \times 1.22 = 0.04$ \\
+ reviews & 1\% & $\sqrt{9/1} = 3$ & $0.03 \times 3 = 0.09$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

With the reviews, almost no rate variation remains that the text cannot predict. Leakage moves the estimate; overlap collapse makes whatever is left rest on a handful of nights.

\medskip

\textbf{Practice}: use only text fixed before the decision, check overlap after adding the embedding, and compare estimates with and without it.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Generative-AI Errors}

\begin{pitfall}{generative-AI errors}
Treating a simulator's realism as causal validity; ranking pipelines on one simulator class; reading a silicon sample as data; validating a language-model label on pre-treatment text only; gold labels from one arm; embeddings of post-treatment text.
\end{pitfall}

\bigskip

Each has a number today:
\begin{itemize}
  \item two simulators reproduce the same six nights and disagree on the sign of the effect;
  \item the vendor's regret is \textyen 380 on its own model class and \textyen 2{,}240 on another;
  \item the synthetic guests' $-0.9$ points the opposite way from every measured number;
  \item the model's false-positive rate was 0.023 on current-rate reviews and 0.073 on higher-rate ones;
  \item a rectifier from the current arm alone, $-0.01$ in both arms, leaves the effect at $0.07$; each arm's own gives $0.03$;
  \item review embeddings move the elasticity from $-1.56$ to $-0.97$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} The elasticity against the break-even $-1.32$; a pipeline's regret against the oracle, which counts only misclassified units, weighted by $|v|$; the rise's effect on true complaints, not on model labels.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} A learned simulator's effects are g-formula effects: consistency, conditional exchangeability given $X$, overlap. Its truth is planted, so it tests methods, not the business; a silicon sample's truth is asserted by the model's priors. A model-coded outcome's effect depends on its error rates, arm by arm.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Estimator testbeds measure bias and coverage on the business's data shape; pipeline testbeds measure regret, trusted only after backtests, within support, across model classes. PPI adds each arm's rectifier to the model's rate. Embeddings of pre-treatment text can be DML controls.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} PPI's variance is $\Var(f)/N + \Var(Y - f)/n$: an SE of 0.023 here, against 0.037 for gold alone. Overlap collapse inflates the SE as $1/\sqrt{\text{share left}}$.
  \item \textbf{Ask where the truth comes from.} Planted truth can check a method; asserted truth checks nothing; only a design measures the business.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
