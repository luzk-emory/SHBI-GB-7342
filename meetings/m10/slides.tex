\section{Block A: Panel data I, basics}

% [RELEASE R1: the assistant, its cost, and the three numbers on Dana's desk]
\begin{frame}{The Store Assistant}

Dana runs store operations at Sanlin Home, a home-goods chain of 120 stores. \textbf{Store Assistant} is a generative-AI assistant on associates' handhelds: stock checks, product answers, and add-on suggestions, in seconds.

\medskip

In week 53 the first \textbf{cohort} of 30 stores went live. A second cohort of 30 is scheduled for week 79. It is now week 78. The flagship store went live much earlier, on its own. It is held out today: a single store that adopted alone needs a different method, which comes later in the course.

\medskip

On her desk is the decision to confirm cohort 2, at \textyen 2{,}500 per store-week (license \textyen 1{,}700, handsets \textyen 300, each store's ``AI champion'' allowance \textyen 500).

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Analyst: cohort 1, weekly sales after going live against before & $+$\textyen 15{,}000 \\
Analyst: cohort 1 against the other stores, after going live & $+$\textyen 41{,}000 \\
Vendor dashboard: ``AI-assisted sales'' in cohort 1 stores & \textyen 38{,}000 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\small Analyst: \emph{``Either way, it pays several times over.''} \quad Vendor: \emph{``Assisted sales are fifteen times the fee.''}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Number That Decides It}

Gross margin on incremental sales is 25\%. The assistant pays in a store-week only if
\[
0.25 \times \Delta\text{sales} \;>\; 2{,}500
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\Delta\text{sales} \;>\; \tau^\ast = \frac{2{,}500}{0.25} = \textbf{\textyen 10{,}000 per store-week}.
\]

Every estimate today is a weekly sales lift per store, in \textyen 000; the question is which side of 10 it lands on.

\bigskip

The dashboard is not a third estimate. It counts every sale in which an associate queried the assistant, including the customer who came in for the frying pan anyway. It is measured only in treated stores and has no counterfactual: usage, not impact (Meeting 1's sure things, in a vendor's report).

\medskip

Even on its own terms, ``fifteen times'' compares sales with cost: $38{,}000/2{,}500 = 15.2$. In margin, $0.25 \times 38{,}000 = 9{,}500$, which is $3.8$ times the fee.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Panel Data and Adoption Dates}

Stores $i$ are observed in weeks $t$. Index potential outcomes by the \textbf{adoption date}: $Y_{it}(g)$ is store $i$'s sales in week $t$ if it first goes live in week $g$; $g = \infty$ means never.

\smallskip

With one adoption date, $t_0 = 53$: cohort 1 ($T_i = 1$) shows $Y_{it}(53)$, the comparison stores ($T_i = 0$) show $Y_{it}(\infty)$. For short, $Y_{it}(1) \equiv Y_{it}(53)$ and $Y_{it}(0) \equiv Y_{it}(\infty)$.

\begin{defbox}{the target}
\[
\ATT = \E\bigl[Y_{\text{post}}(1) - Y_{\text{post}}(0) \mid T = 1\bigr], \qquad \text{post} = \text{weeks 53--78}.
\]
The first term is observed. The difficulty is $\E[Y_{\text{post}}(0) \mid T = 1]$: cohort 1's sales without the assistant.
\end{defbox}

Unlike in Meetings 1--6, no coin chose the go-live dates, and unlike in Meetings 7--8, no recorded rule did. The missing term must come from untreated stores over time, through a claim about what cohort 1 would have done. You can check that claim only in part, so say plainly that identification here rests on an assumption.
\end{frame}

\medskip

The analyst's two numbers come from two different cells of this table:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{before--after}, within cohort 1: $196 - 181 = 15$;
  \item \textbf{across stores}, after going live: $196 - 155 = 41$.

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Before--After Keeps Everything Else That Changed}

A treated unit's before--after change attributes to the treatment \textbf{everything else that changed}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item trends: the chain grows, prices rise;
  \item seasons: ``before'' is the second half of one year, ``after'' the first half of the next;
  \item other events: a chain-wide promotion, a competitor's closure, a new range;
  \item \textbf{regression to the mean}: units chosen because they had a bad period recover anyway.
\end{itemize}

\bigskip

The comparison stores never had the assistant, yet over the same weeks they moved from 151 to 155, a $+4$ that every store saw without it. If cohort 1 would have shared it, the 15 contains it.

\medskip

Before--after needs one assumption: \emph{nothing else changed}. Over half a year in retail it is never true.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Regression to the Mean}

\begin{result}{1.1}{Before--after with selection on a bad period}
Let $Y_{it}(0) = a_i + \lambda_t + u_{it}$ with transitory shocks $u_{it}$ independent over time with mean zero. If units are treated because their pre-period outcome was low, the before--after change among treated units is
\[
  \E[Y_{\text{post}} - Y_{\text{pre}} \mid T = 1] = \ATT + (\lambda_{\text{post}} - \lambda_{\text{pre}}) - \E[u_{\text{pre}} \mid T = 1],
\]
and the last term is positive.
\end{result}

\medskip

Selection uses the pre-period outcome only, so the post-period shock still averages zero; selecting on a low pre-period outcome makes the pre-period shock negative on average.

\medskip

``Fix the worst-performing stores'' builds regression to the mean into the rollout. A comparison group fixes the trend term, but not this one unless it was selected the same way.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Pilot in the Worst Stores, by Numbers}

Suppose Dana had piloted in the stores with the \textbf{worst Q4}. Each store's quarterly sales are its own level $a_i$, plus a chain-wide $\lambda_t$ that rises by 2 a quarter, plus a shock $u_{it}$ of $+4$ or $-4$, equally likely and independent across quarters. The assistant does nothing: $\ATT = 0$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Stores} & \textbf{Q4 shock} & \textbf{Mean Q5 shock} & \textbf{Mean change, Q4 to Q5} \\
\midrule
Pilot (a bad Q4) & $-4$ & 0 & $2 + 4 = 6$ \\
Not piloted (a good Q4) & $+4$ & 0 & $2 - 4 = -2$ \\
Not piloted, also a bad Q4 & $-4$ & 0 & $2 + 4 = 6$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}
  \item Before--after on the pilot: $6 = 0 + 2 + 4$, the trend plus the recovery, from an assistant that does nothing.
  \item Against the stores not piloted, which had the good quarter: $6 - (-2) = 8$. That is worse, because the comparison falls back as the pilot recovers.
  \item Against stores selected the same way: $6 - 6 = 0$. Only a comparison chosen on the same bad quarter removes the recovery.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Across Stores Keeps the Level Gap}

The analyst's other number compares stores at one time: after going live, $196 - 155 = 41$.

\bigskip

But before anyone had the assistant, cohort 1 already sold $181 - 151 = \textbf{30}$ more per week. Its stores are larger, in better locations, or run differently. The cross-section credits the assistant with that gap.

\bigskip

This is Meeting 1's selection bias, $\E[Y(0) \mid T=1] - \E[Y(0) \mid T=0]$, and the panel lets us \emph{see} it, measured before treatment.

\bigskip

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Keeps} \\
\midrule
Before--after, within cohort 1 & what changed over time for every store \\
Across stores, after going live & what differs between stores at every time \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

Fixed effects remove each of the two, one per index.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Fixed Effects}

The two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) model:
\[
Y_{it} = \alpha_i + \lambda_t + \beta D_{it} + \varepsilon_{it}, \qquad D_{it} = \ind{\text{store } i \text{ is live in week } t}.
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item $\alpha_i$, the \textbf{unit effect}: each store's own level. It absorbs every stable characteristic, measured or not, such as floor area, location, catchment, and format.
  \item $\lambda_t$, the \textbf{time effect}: each week's shock common to all stores. It absorbs the chain's trend, the season, and chain-wide events.
  \item $\beta$ is then identified only by how a store's sales move, relative to the chain's week, when its $D_{it}$ switches.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

Variables that never change within a store drop out. The model cannot say what floor area does to sales, and it does not need to: whatever floor area does, $\alpha_i$ holds it.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Fixed Effects Cannot Remove}

$\alpha_i$ is constant over time; $\lambda_t$ is common to all stores. Confounders that change over time \textbf{differently across units} are in neither.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{5.3cm}L{3.0cm}L{4.3cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{At the chain} & \textbf{Kind} & \textbf{What it does to $\hat\beta$} \\
\midrule
Cohort 1's region growing faster: a new housing estate & a differential trend & credits the assistant with the region's growth \\[0.4em]
A region that signed off \emph{because} of a bad quarter & a transitory shock, $u_{it}$ & credits it with the recovery (Result 1.1) \\[0.4em]
A store manager who adopts early and also works harder & store-specific and time-varying & credits it with the manager's effort \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

None is about levels, which $\alpha_i$ handles. Each is a reason the \textbf{timing} of adoption might be related to where a store's sales were heading.

\medskip

And no fixed effect fixes a bad \emph{control}: staff hours re-planned around the assistant are an outcome of the treatment, not a confounder (Meeting 7).
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Within Transformation}

\begin{result}{1.2}{TWFE by the within transformation}
In a balanced panel, with $\ddot W_{it} = W_{it} - \bar W_{i\cdot} - \bar W_{\cdot t} + \bar W_{\cdot\cdot}$, the least-squares coefficient on $D$ is
$\hat\beta = \sum_{i,t}\ddot D_{it}Y_{it}/\sum_{i,t}\ddot D_{it}^2$.
\end{result}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Subtracting each store's mean removes $\alpha_i$. Subtracting each week's mean removes $\lambda_t$. The grand mean, subtracted twice, is added back once.
  \item What is left of $D$ varies within store \emph{and} within week. $\hat\beta$ is the slope of $Y$ on it, by the Frisch--Waugh--Lovell theorem from Meeting 8.
  \item Only variation within units over time identifies $\beta$.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

\textbf{Balanced panels only.} If stores open or close mid-panel, one-pass demeaning is not the projection: run the regression with both sets of indicators.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Four Cells, Two Ways}

Treat the two group means as a balanced panel of two ``units'' over two periods.

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.40\textwidth}
\textbf{Store effects only.} Subtracting each unit's mean leaves $D$ varying only in cohort 1 (0 before, 1 after), so
\[
\hat\beta = 196 - 181 = 15,
\]
the before--after. The time trend is still in it.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.56\textwidth}
\textbf{Store and period effects.} Double-demeaned:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{lrr|rr}
\toprule
& \multicolumn{2}{c|}{$\ddot Y$} & \multicolumn{2}{c}{$\ddot D$} \\
& Before & After & Before & After \\
\midrule
Cohort 1 & $-2.75$ & $2.75$ & $-0.25$ & $0.25$ \\
Comparison & $2.75$ & $-2.75$ & $0.25$ & $-0.25$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\[
\hat\beta = \frac{\sum \ddot D\,Y}{\sum \ddot D^{\,2}} = \frac{0.25\,(-181 + 196 + 151 - 155)}{4 \times 0.0625} = \frac{2.75}{0.25} = 11.
\]

The time effects bring in the comparison's $+4$: $15 - 4 = 11$. The unit effects had already removed the level gap of 30. Whether 11 is the assistant's effect depends on an assumption, the subject of Block B.
\end{frame}

  \item Across stores keeps the level gap; 41 includes the 30 that was there before.

\section{Block B: Panel data II, difference-in-differences}
% ====================================================================

\begin{frame}{What Must Be True}

\begin{assumption}{1.3}{Parallel trends}
$\E[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid T=1] = \E[Y_{\text{post}}(0) - Y_{\text{pre}}(0) \mid T=0]$.
\end{assumption}

\begin{assumption}{1.4}{No anticipation and no spillovers}
$Y_{\text{pre}}(1) = Y_{\text{pre}}(0)$ for treated units, and comparison units' outcomes do not depend on whether treated units are treated.
\end{assumption}

\medskip

\begin{itemize}
  \item Parallel trends restricts \textbf{changes}, not levels: cohort 1 may be 30 bigger, provided the difference would have stayed 30.
  \item It is untestable: cohort 1's post-period $Y(0)$ is never seen.
  \item No anticipation fixes what ``pre'' means. If behavior changed when adoption was announced, or while staff prepared for it, the pre-period must end before that.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{The Rollout File, Sentence by Sentence}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{L{6.6cm}L{6.4cm}}
\toprule
\textbf{The file says} & \textbf{Which supports, or puts at risk} \\
\midrule
``Cohorts were assigned by region, in the order the regional managers signed off, after each region's data-privacy review.'' & Timing was not chosen by a store; but parallel trends becomes a claim about \emph{regions}. \\[0.4em]
``Associates were trained on the assistant in weeks 51--52, in paid sessions held off the shop floor.'' & No anticipation, \emph{if} training did not thin service. If it did, weeks 51--52 are depressed and the DiD is biased up. \\[0.4em]
``The comparison stores are the 89 whose regions had not yet signed off.'' & Same format and calendar; their levels do not matter. \\[0.4em]
``Licenses are tied to store handsets; no two stores share a catchment.'' & No spillovers. \\[0.4em]
``Once the assistant took over stock checks, store managers re-planned their rotas.'' & A warning: staff hours are now caused by the assistant. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\medskip

\small The first sentence matters most: if a region's place in the sign-off order tracked where its sales were heading, parallel trends fails.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Difference-in-Differences}

\begin{result}{1.5}{DiD identifies the ATT}
Under Assumptions 1.3--1.4,
\[
  \ATT = \{\E[Y_{\text{post}} \mid T=1] - \E[Y_{\text{pre}} \mid T=1]\} - \{\E[Y_{\text{post}} \mid T=0] - \E[Y_{\text{pre}} \mid T=0]\}.
\]
\end{result}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
\textbf{Comparison} & \textbf{Arithmetic} & \textbf{\textyen 000} \\
\midrule
Before--after & $196 - 181$ & \textcolor{warnred}{15} \\
Across stores & $196 - 155$ & \textcolor{warnred}{41} \\
\textbf{Difference-in-differences} & $(196 - 181) - (155 - 151)$ & \textcolor{sbgreen}{\textbf{11}} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\[
15 = 11 + \underbrace{4}_{\text{what every store did anyway}}, \qquad 41 = 11 + \underbrace{30}_{\text{cohort 1 was bigger before}} .
\]

The before--after keeps the common trend; the cross-section keeps the level gap. The DiD removes both. At \textbf{11} against a break-even of 10, it clears by \textyen 1{,}000, not ``several times over''.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Parallel in Yuan, or in Percent?}

\begin{result}{1.6}{Parallel trends depends on the scale}
If parallel trends holds in levels with a common counterfactual change $\Delta \ne 0$ and the groups' pre-period means differ, it fails in logs (approximately, in percentage changes).
\end{result}

\smallskip

Here $\Delta = 4$ on pre-period means of 181 and 151: parallel in yuan means cohort 1 would have grown $4/181 = 2.2\%$ while the comparison grew $4/151 = 2.6\%$.

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llr}
\toprule
\textbf{Counterfactual for cohort 1} & \textbf{Arithmetic} & \textbf{Effect} \\
\midrule
Same change in yuan & $181 + 4 = 185$ & $196 - 185 = 11.0$ \\
Same change in percent & $181 \times 155/151 = 185.8$ & $196 - 185.8 = 10.2$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The choice moves about \textyen 800 of a \textyen 1{,}000 margin. Choose the scale on which common shocks plausibly act, and check which one the pre-period supports: the Q1--Q4 gaps are flat in yuan (30 every quarter), while in percent they shrink from $20.5\%$ to $19.7\%$. Use yuan, and report the other, because the decision is close.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{With One Adoption Date, TWFE Is the DiD}

\begin{result}{1.7}{With one adoption date, TWFE is the DiD}
In a balanced panel with $n_1$ treated and $n_0$ comparison units, $\mathcal T_0$ pre- and $\mathcal T_1$ post-periods, and
$\Delta_i = \bar Y_{i,\text{post}} - \bar Y_{i,\text{pre}}$, the TWFE coefficient is $\hat\beta = \bar\Delta_{T=1} - \bar\Delta_{T=0}$.
\end{result}

\begin{frame}{Flat Pre-Trends Are Weak Evidence}

Three reasons a flat pre-period does not prove parallel trends:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Power.} With few, noisy pre-periods a test can miss a drift large enough to explain the whole ``effect''.
  \item \textbf{Selection on the test.} Reporting only analyses that pass it biases what gets reported.
  \item \textbf{Timing.} A shock that coincides with adoption, such as a regional campaign launched the same week, leaves pre-trends untouched.
\end{itemize}

\medskip

To see how much drift the pre-period can rule out, suppose a linear drift of $\delta$ per quarter, so that $\beta_{-2} = -\delta$, $\beta_{-3} = -2\delta$, and $\beta_{-4} = -3\delta$. Fit $\delta$ to the three pre-period coefficients by least squares, treating them as independent with SE $1.22$ each:
\[
\hat\delta = -\frac{1 \cdot \hat\beta_{-2} + 2\,\hat\beta_{-3} + 3\,\hat\beta_{-4}}{1 + 4 + 9} = 0, \qquad
\SE(\hat\delta) = \frac{1.22}{\sqrt{14}} = 0.33 .
\]
The 95\% interval is $[-0.64,\ 0.64]$. Perfectly flat coefficients still allow a drift of \textyen 640 per store-week per quarter in either direction.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Breakdown Drift}

\begin{result}{1.8}{Linear differential trend and the breakdown drift}
If the counterfactual gap drifts linearly, $\E[Y_t(0) \mid T=1] - \E[Y_t(0) \mid T=0] = a + \delta t$, a DiD comparing post periods with mean
$\bar t_{\text{post}}$ to pre periods with mean $\bar t_{\text{pre}}$ converges to $\ATT + \delta(\bar t_{\text{post}} - \bar t_{\text{pre}})$. Against a break-even $c$,
the decision changes at the \emph{breakdown drift} $\delta^\ast = (\text{DiD} - c)/(\bar t_{\text{post}} - \bar t_{\text{pre}})$.
\end{result}

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
\textbf{Estimate} & \textbf{Compares} & \textbf{DiD} & $\bar t_{\text{post}} - \bar t_{\text{pre}}$ & $\delta^\ast$ \\
\midrule
Four cells & Q5--Q6 with Q3--Q4 & 11 & $5.5 - 3.5 = 2$ & $(11 - 10)/2 = 0.5$ \\
TWFE, six quarters & Q5--Q6 with Q1--Q4 & 11 & $5.5 - 2.5 = 3$ & $(11 - 10)/3 = 0.33$ \\
Event study, $\hat\beta_1$ & Q6 with Q4 & 14 & $6 - 4 = 2$ & $(14 - 10)/2 = 2.0$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The pre-period rules out drifts beyond $0.64$ only. The average margin does not survive a drift it cannot rule out (0.5 moves the Q1--Q4 gap by just $1.5$). The second-quarter effect needs a drift of 2.0, three times what the pre-period allows. Report $\delta^\ast$, and argue from the sign-off process whether a regional drift that large is plausible.
\end{frame}

% [RELEASE R4: store-weeks and serial correlation (the case part is to be rewritten to this frame's AR(1) numbers)]
\begin{frame}{Store-Weeks Are Not Independent}

The panel has $119 \times 78 = 9{,}282$ store-weeks. A store's sales this week predict its sales next week, and $D_{it}$ is constant within long blocks. A standard error that treats store-weeks as independent counts the same store many times.

\bigskip

To see how much too small, suppose a store's weekly shocks, net of its level and the chain's week, have variance $\sigma^2$ and follow an AR(1) with autocorrelation $0.8$. Its change $\Delta_i$, after-mean minus before-mean over 26 weeks each, then has
\[
\Var(\Delta_i) = 6.70 \times \sigma^2\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{26} + \tfrac{1}{26}\Bigr),
\]
where $\sigma^2(\frac1{26} + \frac1{26})$ is what independence implies. The independent-weeks standard error is too small by $\sqrt{6.70} = 2.59$.

\bigskip

The fix does not need the AR(1), or any model of the correlation over time: give each store \textbf{one} number.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{One Change per Store}

\begin{result}{1.9}{Collapsing to one change per unit}
If units are independent, $\bar\Delta_{T=1} - \bar\Delta_{T=0}$ has $\SE = \sqrt{s_1^2/n_1 + s_0^2/n_0}$ with $s_d$ the standard deviation of $\Delta_i$ in
group $d$, whatever the correlation of a unit's outcomes over time.
\end{result}

\medskip

Store-level changes have standard deviations of 6 in cohort 1 and 5 in the comparison:
\[
\SE = \sqrt{\tfrac{6^2}{30} + \tfrac{5^2}{89}} = \sqrt{1.20 + 0.28} = 1.22, \qquad 11 \pm 1.96 \times 1.22 = [8.6,\ 13.4] .
\]

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{It straddles 10.} In margin, $0.25 \times \text{lift} - 2{,}500$ runs from $-$\textyen 350 to $+$\textyen 850 per store-week.
  \item The independent-weeks SE would be $1.22/2.59 = 0.47$, with interval $[10.1,\ 11.9]$: clear of break-even, falsely.
  \item With one adoption date, this is asymptotically the TWFE SE clustered by store.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{Cluster Where Treatment Was Assigned}

Cohorts were assigned \textbf{by region}, and regions share shocks: a regional promotion, a local closure, a heatwave. Then stores in a region are not independent either. Cluster at the level at which treatment was assigned and shocks are shared.

\bigskip

Cohort 1 is in three regions; the comparison stores are in nine. Region-level changes have standard deviations of 3 and 2.5:
\[
\SE = \sqrt{\tfrac{3^2}{3} + \tfrac{2.5^2}{9}} = \sqrt{3.00 + 0.69} = 1.92, \qquad 11 \pm 2.23 \times 1.92 = [6.7,\ 15.3],
\]
with the $t$ critical value for $3 + 9 - 2 = 10$ degrees of freedom. In margin, that is $-$\textyen 825 to $+$\textyen 1{,}325 per store-week.

\bigskip

With few clusters, use $t$ critical values with cluster-based degrees of freedom, or the wild cluster bootstrap. Twelve clusters give an honest interval, and a wide one.
\end{frame}

% ====================================================================
\begin{frame}{A Warning on Staggered Timing}

Cohort 2 goes live in week 79, the start of Q7. At the next review, stores will have several adoption dates, and TWFE with a single $D_{it}$ still runs. It no longer equals one clean DiD: it averages many two-by-two comparisons, some using \textbf{already-treated} stores as controls for later adopters.

\bigskip

Take one such comparison, cohort 2 against cohort 1 from Q6 to Q7. Suppose parallel trends holds exactly, cohort 2's first-quarter effect is 8, as cohort 1's was, and cohort 1's own effect is still growing, from 14 to 18. Then the comparison gives
\[
8 - (18 - 14) = 4, \qquad \text{not } 8 .
\]
The growth in the control's effect is subtracted.

\bigskip

If effects grow over time, such comparisons subtract part of the effect, some get \textbf{negative weight}, and the estimate can be badly biased, even of the wrong sign. Use a design built for staggered timing: compare each cohort only with not-yet-treated or never-treated stores, and report effects by cohort.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Summary}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \spinestep{Estimand.} $\ATT = \E[Y_{\text{post}}(1) - Y_{\text{post}}(0) \mid T = 1]$, with adoption-date potential outcomes $Y_{it}(g)$, against a break-even of 10. A vendor's usage count is not an estimate of it.
  \item \spinestep{Identification.} No coin chose the dates. Parallel trends (on a stated scale), no anticipation, and no spillovers are assumptions, tied to why the treated adopted when they did. Fixed effects remove stable levels and common shocks, not differential trends or a dip before adoption.
  \item \spinestep{Estimation.} Before--after keeps the trend (15), across keeps the level (41), the DiD removes both (11). With one adoption date, TWFE is the DiD; an event study traces the effect (8, then 14) and gives placebo pre-period coefficients.
  \item \spinestep{Inference.} One change per store (SE 1.22, not 0.47); cluster where treatment was assigned (by region, $[6.7, 15.3]$). Report the breakdown drift (0.5 for the average, 2.0 for the second quarter) against what the pre-period rules out (0.64).
  \item \textbf{With several adoption dates, TWFE can weight effects negatively.} Use a design built for staggered timing.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
